Calcium nitrate as a latex coagulant: how it is used in glove and dipped-rubber manufacturing

Last updated: 7 October 2026  ·  Reviewed by Sujay Shrivastava - Digital Transformation Consultant  ·  20 cited sources (16 high-authority)
In summary

Calcium nitrate is the most commonly used coagulant in dipped latex products such as examination gloves. A hand-shaped former is dipped into an aqueous calcium nitrate solution and dried; when it is then dipped into liquid latex, the calcium ions destabilise the latex particles next to the former so they aggregate into a soft “wet gel” film that is later dried and cured into the glove.[1][2][7] Published patent examples use roughly 250–300 g/L calcium nitrate in a first coagulant dip and 300–400 g/L in a second, both at 60–75 °C.[8] Gloves are the best-documented use, but the same coagulant-dip principle applies to other dipped latex goods.

What a latex coagulant does

Liquid latex is a stable colloid: tiny rubber particles are kept apart by surface charge and surfactant. To build a thin solid film on a mould, that stability has to be broken exactly where you want rubber to deposit. A coagulant is an electrolyte that does this. Calcium nitrate supplies divalent calcium ions (Ca2+) that diffuse out of the dried coating on the former into the latex, and where their concentration is high enough the particles flocculate and stick to the former.[2][4] The nitrate ion is the soluble, non-chloride counter-ion that carries the calcium.[1]

The coagulant-dipping process, step by step

The sequence below is the published, generic coagulant dipping route used for thin examination gloves.[7][8]

  1. Clean the former. Ceramic hand-shaped formers are washed and conditioned.[8]
  2. Coagulant dip. The former is dipped in an aqueous solution containing calcium nitrate (commonly with a wetting agent and an anti-tack/release agent).[7][8][13]
  3. Dry. The coagulant layer is dried onto the former so a defined amount of salt sits on the surface.[7]
  4. Latex dip. The former is dipped into a dilute latex compound (about 10% solids). The coagulant diffuses into the latex and a wet-gel layer builds up; thickness grows with dwell time.[2][4][7]
  5. Optional second coagulant dip and second latex dip for layered or thicker products; the second coagulant is typically more concentrated to speed gelation and bond the layers.[8]
  6. Leach, dry and cure. The film is leached to remove residual salts, dried and cured, then stripped from the former.[7][13]

Real lines tune concentration, temperature and dwell for the latex grade, glove thickness and former speed, and often use calcium carbonate or stearate as a former-release agent alongside the coagulant.[10][13] Always develop your own set-points on your own line.

Why calcium nitrate rather than another salt

  • Very high water solubility — roughly 1,200 g/L at 20 °C for the anhydrous salt — so strong coagulant baths can be made up without precipitation.[1][17]
  • No chloride. Calcium chloride is also studied for glove latex,[5] but a chloride-free coagulant avoids chloride carry-over into the product and equipment corrosion in the dip line.
  • Divalent cation. Divalent ions such as Ca2+ are far more effective at destabilising latex than monovalent ions at the same molar concentration (the Schulze–Hardy rule of colloid chemistry).

Quality issues linked to the coagulant stage

  • Residual calcium nitrate on the glove. Leaching must remove it; a 2020 Moratuwa study links residual calcium nitrate/calcium ions and release agent to a “wet look” defect in thick nitrile gloves.[13]
  • Uneven coagulant film. Non-uniform coagulant deposition gives thickness variation and pinholes; pinhole testing is a core acceptance test for examination gloves.[6][15]
  • Insoluble particles in the coagulant tank deposit on formers and become defects — the practical reason a fully soluble salt matters.
  • Dipping-tank coagulum. Coagulated latex in the dip tanks is scheduled waste in Malaysia and must be disposed of or recycled under regulation.[11]

Regulation and standards context

In the United States, patient examination gloves are regulated medical devices under 21 CFR 880.6250,[12] and nitrile examination gloves are commonly tested to ASTM D6319 for dimensions, physical properties and freedom from holes.[15] Natural rubber latex gloves carry a protein-allergy risk that NIOSH has documented, which is one reason synthetic nitrile latex is widely used.[14] The coagulant is a process aid; its residue is controlled through leaching and final-product testing.

What to ask a calcium nitrate supplier for latex dipping

  1. Calcium (Ca) and nitrogen content, and the hydrate/moisture form, so you can dose accurately by calcium.
  2. Insoluble residue (should be zero or near zero) — request a Certificate of Analysis per batch.
  3. Chloride, sulphate and heavy-metal limits that match your product specification.
  4. Whether a pH-adjusted or additive-ready blend is needed, since coagulant baths may be pH-adjusted.
  5. A sample for a small-scale dip trial before committing a line.

When calcium nitrate is NOT the right choice

  • As a drop-in without a trial. Coagulant concentration, additives and leach conditions are line-specific; run a pilot first.[8][13]
  • Foam, cast-film or non-dipped latex processes that coagulate by heat or acid rather than ionic dipping are different routes.
  • If your specification demands low-chloride, trace-metal-controlled or pharma-grade salt you must confirm the exact limits in writing before ordering any grade.

Frequently asked questions

What does calcium nitrate do in latex gloves?

It is the coagulant. Dried onto the former before the latex dip, it releases calcium ions that make latex particles aggregate into a wet-gel film on the former, which becomes the glove.[2][7]

How much calcium nitrate is used in a coagulant dip?

Patent examples cite roughly 250–300 g/L for a first coagulant dip and 300–400 g/L for a second, at 60–75 °C. Actual values are tuned per line, so treat these as published examples, not a specification.[8]

Is calcium nitrate used for nitrile and natural rubber latex?

Yes. Coagulant dipping with calcium nitrate is described for both natural rubber and nitrile latex gloves.[2][7][8]

Why not use calcium chloride instead?

Calcium chloride is also studied for glove latex,[5] but calcium nitrate is the most commonly cited coagulant, avoids adding chloride to the line and dissolves to very concentrated solutions.[1][7]

What defects can the coagulant cause?

Residual coagulant on the glove, uneven coagulant films and insoluble particles in the coagulant tank can lead to surface defects, thickness variation and pinholes. Leaching and quality testing control this.[6][13]
