How do you use calcium nitrate in drip irrigation and fertigation?

Last updated: 7 October 2026  ·  Reviewed by Sujay Shrivastava - Digital Transformation Consultant  ·  20 cited sources (17 high-authority)
In summary

Calcium nitrate is one of the few fully soluble calcium sources for drip systems, and it also supplies nitrate nitrogen. Dissolve it in its own stock tank and inject it through a metered injector; do not put it in a concentrated stock with phosphates or sulphates, which form insoluble calcium phosphate or gypsum that plugs emitters.[2][5] To size the stock, work backwards from the target: at a 1:100 injector, 150 mg/L calcium in the line needs about 7.9 kg of 19%-Ca calcium nitrate per 100 L of stock (worked example below). Hard, alkaline water (about 2 meq/L bicarbonate and pH above 7.5) can precipitate calcium carbonate by itself, so test the water and acidify if needed.[4] Run the injector for at least 30–45 minutes and flush the lines for 30 minutes afterwards.[2]

Why is calcium nitrate used for drip fertigation?

Calcium nitrate supplies calcium and nitrate nitrogen in one soluble product. Extension guidance for blossom-end rot in tomato and pepper recommends delivering soluble calcium through the drip line, because foliar calcium sprays gave inconclusive results.[8] Calcium is only one part of a fertigation program: nitrogen, potassium and acidity are managed alongside it, and calcium nitrate is usually one tank in a multi-product setup. This page covers the delivery mechanics. For the crop physiology of calcium disorders see calcium nitrate for bitter pit and blossom-end rot, and for recirculating soilless systems see calcium nitrate in hydroponics.

Fertigation also saves inputs. A 2024 review from Punjab Agricultural University reports that applying fertilizer through drip saves about 20% of fertilizer and 20–35% of irrigation water across fruit crops.[14]

As arithmetic, 20 lb N/week from a 15.5% N product is about 129 lb of product per acre, which also carries about 24 lb Ca at 19% Ca. Very high ammonium supply can worsen calcium disorders by competing with calcium uptake, which is why the Georgia schedule rotates calcium nitrate with other fertilizers.[7]

How do you calculate the stock and injector ratio?

Extension calculators use one idea: parts per million of a nutrient in the final water equals the concentration in the stock divided by the injector ratio.[5][16] Work from the target to the stock:

  1. Choose the target in the line. Example: 150 mg/L calcium.
  2. Multiply by the ratio. At 1:100 the stock must hold 150 × 100 = 15,000 mg/L = 15 g Ca per litre.[16]
  3. Convert to product. At 19% Ca, 15 ÷ 0.19 = 78.9 g per litre, or 7.9 kg per 100 L of stock (8.1 kg at 18.5% Ca).
  4. Check the nitrogen delivered. 78.9 g × 0.155 = 12.2 g N per litre of stock, so about 122 mg/L N in the line at 1:100. This is a side effect you must count in your nitrogen budget.
  5. Change ratio, not chemistry. At 1:200 the same final concentration needs twice the stock strength, about 15.8 kg per 100 L, which is still far below the salt's solubility.[16][18]

A published worked example gives the same method: 166 ppm Ca from 19% Ca product is 330 g per 100 US gallons of final solution, which also delivers about 135 ppm N at 15.5% N.[6] Mississippi State's injector bulletin uses the same ppm formulas and notes that fertilizer must be measured accurately because injector systems are precise.[5] Weigh fertilizer rather than measuring by volume.[16]

An older HRSU guide quoted stock tanks of 10–20 kg per 100 L. We could not tie that range to a published source. It is best read as common practice: the real limit is set by your injector ratio and demand, not by solubility, because calcium nitrate dissolves at over 1,000 g/L.[18] Mississippi State notes that solubility limits are not a problem for most common greenhouse fertilizers.[5]

Does calcium nitrate dissolve slowly or cool the tank?

Solubility rises with temperature, from about 1,050 g/L at 0 °C to 1,290 g/L at 20 °C.[18] Dissolving the tetrahydrate is endothermic, which is why it is used in cold packs, so a big batch in cold water cools the tank and can slow dissolution.[18] Our source for the endothermic behaviour is encyclopedic only, so we advise watching the tank temperature rather than relying on a number. If something will not dissolve, stir with a pump or mixer, or use warm water; Mississippi State suggests circulating or mechanical mixing, or hot water, when solubility problems arise.[5] Do not let a cold-night stock sit with undissolved solids reaching the injector.

Why does calcium nitrate need its own tank, and what precipitates?

Calcium forms three insoluble solids that matter in drip lines. Keep them in mind when you plan tanks.

  • Calcium phosphate. Calcium with phosphorus in a concentrate forms calcium hydrogen phosphate, described as a very hard precipitate that clogs injectors and irrigation systems; in diluted solutions the reaction does not occur.[5] Florida extension also lists tomato fertigation with calcium nitrate as less common because of phosphate precipitation and advises keeping combined calcium and magnesium below 50 ppm where phosphate is present.[1]
  • Gypsum (calcium sulphate). Calcium injected with any sulphate fertilizer forms gypsum, and fertilizers containing calcium should be flushed from tanks, pumps, filters and tubing before phosphorus, urea-ammonium nitrate or urea-sulphuric fertilizer is injected.[3] Gypsum dissolves only to about 2–2.5 g/L.[19]
  • Calcium carbonate. Hard water can scale emitters even with no fertilizer: bicarbonate above about 2 meq/L with pH above about 7.5 is the warning zone, and adding calcium can raise the risk.[4]

Tank labels differ between sources. Mississippi State calls the calcium nitrate tank “B” and the tank for other nutrients “A”,[5] while many growers reverse the letters. The rule is the same: calcium nitrate and its compatible partners in one tank, phosphates and sulphates in another. UF/IFAS lists calcium nitrate as compatible with ammonium, magnesium and potassium nitrate,[2] and Utah State warns that phosphate and sulphate forms precipitate with calcium;[3] the UC compatibility chart is worth checking before any new mix.[17]

Should you acidify the water?

Acid can help in two different ways. In alkaline, bicarbonate-rich water, K-State advises lowering pH to 7.0 or below by continuous injection to stop calcium carbonate scale.[4] For soilless greenhouse tomato, Mississippi State uses an extra injector head to hold solution pH at 5.6–5.8 when source water is above 5.8.[5] Florida extension lists hydrochloric, sulphuric, citric and phosphoric acid as options and advises flushing after acid injection to limit precipitation and corrosion.[1] Choose the acid with the rest of the program in mind: phosphoric acid must not be mixed with calcium fertilizer because it forms insoluble calcium phosphate, and sulphuric acid adds sulphate that can combine with calcium downstream.[2][3] Use acid from its own tank and handle it under its safety data sheet.

How do you prevent filtration and clogging problems?

  • Test the water first. Florida guidance flags suspended solids above 50 ppm and bacterial counts above 2,600 CFU as clogging risks.[1] K-State lists pH, hardness, bicarbonate, iron, sulphide and solids among the tests to run.[4]
  • Size filtration to the emitter. A general rule is to remove particles one-tenth the size of the smallest emitter opening.[4]
  • Jar test before every new mix. Mix fertilizer with irrigation water and watch for precipitate or milkiness for 1–2 hours.[2] North Carolina advice also suggests a small jar batch of the intended mix.[12]
  • Flush. Run clean water for at least 30 minutes after fertigation.[2]
  • Use a fully soluble grade. Undissolved fertilizer settles and can clog emitters.[5] Check the insoluble-residue figure on the COA for any product, powder or granular. See powder vs granular calcium nitrate.
  • Protect the water supply. Install backflow prevention and check valves before injecting chemicals into any irrigation system.[10]

What EC should the fertigation water have?

Our earlier page gave a feed EC of 1.5–2.5 dS/m. That range is typical practice for many fruiting vegetables but we could not source it as a standard, so use your crop's own guidance. What is published is the yield threshold of the crop to salinity: tomato loses no yield up to ECe 2.5 dS/m in the root zone, which corresponds to ECw 1.7 dS/m for irrigation water, and cucumber has the same thresholds.[9] Fertilizer adds to water EC, so measure EC of the final line and compare it to the crop's tolerance and your leaching practice.

How should injections be scheduled?

  • Split nitrogen into frequent small doses; Georgia schedules use weekly rates with peaks mid-season and at least three applications per week for cabbage.[7]
  • Inject for at least 30–45 minutes inside the irrigation cycle so nutrients reach the whole system, then flush.[2]
  • Time calcium for the demand window: from bloom to golf-ball-size fruit in tomato and pepper.[8] Also keep soil moisture steady, because irregular water supply and low soil pH contribute to blossom-end rot.[11][12]
  • Do not stack urea on a high-pH wet surface without irrigating afterwards; see the alternatives table.

Common mistakes

  • Premixing calcium nitrate with MAP, MKP or magnesium sulphate in one concentrate.[5]
  • Injecting phosphoric acid into a line carrying calcium.[2]
  • Skipping the jar test with new water or a new product.[2]
  • Ending the cycle on fertilizer instead of flushing.[2]
  • Ignoring bicarbonate and pH, then blaming the fertilizer for scale.[4]
  • Treating a published N rate as a calcium rate: calcium nitrate is only about 15.5% N.

When calcium nitrate is NOT the right choice

  • Do not mix with phosphates, sulphates or thiosulphates in a concentrate. Insoluble calcium phosphate or gypsum plugs injectors and emitters.[2][5]
  • Not a complete nitrogen program. It is about 15.5% N and 19% Ca; high-N crops need other nitrogen sources, while excess ammonium can compete with calcium.[7]
  • Not a fix for hard, bicarbonate-rich water. Scaling needs acidification and monitoring, and adding calcium can increase the carbonate risk.[4]
  • Not a substitute for water management. Blossom-end rot also follows drought stress and low soil pH, so calcium alone may not cure it.[12]

Frequently asked questions

Does calcium nitrate clog drip emitters?

A fully soluble product dissolves without residue, but clogging can still come from mixing with phosphates or sulphates, from hard alkaline water, or from undissolved solids.[4][5] Run a jar test and flush after injection.[2]

Can I mix calcium nitrate with other fertilizers in one tank?

Only with compatible nitrates such as ammonium, magnesium and potassium nitrate; never with phosphates, sulphates or thiosulphates in a concentrate.[2]

How do I calculate how much calcium nitrate to put in the stock tank?

Multiply the target mg/L of calcium by the injector ratio, then divide by the Ca fraction of the product. At 1:100, 150 mg/L Ca and 19% Ca gives about 7.9 kg per 100 L.[5][16]

What EC should my calcium nitrate feed be?

There is no single standard. A tomato crop tolerates root-zone ECe 2.5 dS/m (ECw 1.7 dS/m) without yield loss, so keep the final feed within your crop's tolerance and verify at the dripper.[9]

Why does the calcium nitrate tank get cold?

Dissolving calcium nitrate tetrahydrate is endothermic, as in cold packs, so a large batch cools the water and may dissolve slowly; stir or circulate and use warmer water if needed.[5][18]

Do I need to acidify water when using calcium nitrate?

If the water has more than about 2 meq/L bicarbonate and pH above 7.5, scale is likely and K-State advises acidifying to pH 7.0 or lower.[4] Never use phosphoric acid in a line carrying calcium.[2]

How long should I inject and flush?

Inject for at least 30–45 minutes and then flush with clean water for at least 30 minutes.[2]

Is calcium nitrate better than calcium chloride or gypsum for drip?

It supplies nitrate N as well as calcium and dissolves far more than gypsum (about 2–2.5 g/L), whereas calcium chloride adds chloride and no nitrogen.[11][18][19]
