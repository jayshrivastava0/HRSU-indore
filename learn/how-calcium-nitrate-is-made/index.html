How calcium nitrate is manufactured: limestone and nitric acid, the nitrophosphate (Odda) route, and how the commercial forms are made

Last updated: 7 October 2026  ·  Reviewed by Sujay Shrivastava - Digital Transformation Consultant  ·  22 cited sources (13 high-authority)
In summary

Calcium nitrate is made by neutralising nitric acid with limestone or lime: CaCO3 + 2 HNO3 → Ca(NO3)2 + CO2 + H2O.[18] It is also produced as a by-product of the nitrophosphate (Odda) process, where phosphate rock is digested in about 60–62% nitric acid and calcium nitrate tetrahydrate is crystallised out by cooling.[1][14] The solution is then filtered, usually neutralised with a little ammonia, evaporated and formed into prills, granules, flakes or powder; commercial prilled and granulated material is typically 77–80% Ca(NO3)2, 6–8% ammonium nitrate and 14–17% crystal water.[2] The product was first made at industrial scale in Norway from 1905 using the Birkeland–Eyde electric-arc process.[11][15]

What is the basic chemistry of making calcium nitrate?

Calcium nitrate is the calcium salt of nitric acid. The simplest industrial route is acid–base neutralisation: limestone (calcium carbonate) or lime is reacted with nitric acid, giving calcium nitrate, carbon dioxide and water.[18] A third route in the literature reacts ammonium nitrate with calcium hydroxide in aqueous solution.[18] Because the salt is extremely soluble (121.2 g/100 mL of water, about 1,200 g/L),[12] the product stays dissolved and the work of manufacture is mostly removing water and forming a handleable solid.

Two simple mass-balance figures follow from the formula (my own arithmetic from molar masses, not a source value): making 1 tonne of anhydrous Ca(NO3)2 needs about 0.77 t of 100% HNO3 and about 0.61 t of CaCO3, and releases about 0.27 t of CO2. Nitric acid is therefore the dominant raw-material cost and the main environmental load, which is why the nitric-acid chain is covered below.

How is calcium nitrate made from limestone and nitric acid, step by step?

Patent literature describes the commercial sequence for the limestone route. A calcium nitrate solution is produced by neutralising about 60% nitric acid with limestone, then neutralising with ammonia so that roughly one ammonium nitrate is formed for every five calcium nitrate (a molar ratio of about 5:1).[4] A published example filters the solution to remove insoluble matter before ammoniation and concentrates it by evaporation.[4][7] Older patents show that neutralisation of nitric acid with lime-based materials has been used for this purpose for about a century.[7]

  1. Neutralise. Limestone (or lime) is added to about 60% nitric acid; CO2 is released.[4][18]
  2. Clarify. The hot solution is filtered to remove insoluble matter from the stone.[4]
  3. Optional ammoniation. A small amount of ammonia converts part of the nitrate to ammonium nitrate, giving about a 5:1 Ca:NH4 molar ratio.[4]
  4. Concentrate. Water is evaporated; granulation feeds in the patent example are 85–90% m/m.[4]
  5. Form the solid. Prilling, fluidised-bed or pan granulation, flaking, or drying to powder (see forms below).[2][4][8]
  6. Cool, screen and pack. Granules are cooled below 40 °C in the patent example before bagging; dry air is used because the product is hygroscopic.[4][22]

What is the Odda (nitrophosphate) process and why does it make calcium nitrate?

In the nitrophosphate route, phosphate rock is dissolved in nitric acid instead of sulphuric acid. In the GNFC plant described to IFA, rock is digested in about 60–62% nitric acid, sand is removed, and the solution of phosphoric acid, calcium nitrate and excess nitric acid is cooled in batch crystallisers so that calcium nitrate crystallises as the tetrahydrate; the crystals are filtered and washed to remove adherent phosphoric and nitric acid.[14] The EU BAT reference document describes the same logic: cooling separates most calcium from the digest, and the route avoids the gypsum formed in sulphuric-acid processes.[1] The process is credited to Erling Johnson at Odda, Norway; sources differ on whether the date is the late 1920s or 1930s.[14][19]

What happens to the calcium nitrate is a commercial choice. The BAT document says it is either converted to ammonium nitrate and calcium carbonate for CAN fertiliser or, after purification, marketed as calcium nitrate fertiliser.[1] Five nitrophosphate plants were operating in Europe when that document was written, including one at Porsgrunn.[1] Norwegian sources describe Herøya near Porsgrunn as one of the world’s most significant calcium nitrate production sites.[15] The BAT document also notes that calcium nitrate can alternatively be produced by neutralising nitric acid with lime.[1]

What was the Birkeland–Eyde process and Norgessalpeter?

Before ammonia synthesis, nitrogen was fixed with electricity. Kristian Birkeland and Sam Eyde tested an electric-arc process in 1903 at what is now the University of Oslo; air passed through an arc formed nitrogen oxides, which were converted to nitric acid.[9][20] Hydro dates its company history from 1905,[16] and calcium nitrate was sold from 1905 under the name Norgessalpeter (Norwegian saltpetre), containing roughly 13% nitrogen, the first artificial nitrogen fertiliser made from air at industrial scale.[15] The Rjukan–Notodden industrial complex, built to make fertiliser from atmospheric nitrogen, was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2015.[11]

The method was abandoned because it was energy-hungry: published estimates are about 2.4–3.1 MJ per mol of nitrogen fixed at 1–2 mol% NO in the gas, against about 0.5–0.6 MJ per mol N for Haber–Bosch ammonia.[9] Norsk Hydro moved to ammonia-based production around 1928–1929 and introduced kalksalpeter, a calcium nitrate–ammonium nitrate double salt that was less hygroscopic than pure calcium nitrate.[15]

The “15.5-0-0 + 19% Ca” horticultural product corresponds closely to the double salt 5Ca(NO3)2·NH4NO3·10H2O. Calculated from that formula (my arithmetic, not a source figure), the pure double salt is about 18.5% Ca and 15.5% N, of which about 1.3 points is ammoniacal. Double salts of calcium nitrate and ammonium nitrate have been patented and studied for roughly a century,[5][6] and the decahydrate has been characterised as a defined crystalline phase.[5] Pure tetrahydrate, by the same arithmetic, is only about 17% Ca and 12% N, which is one reason commercial fertiliser products are not simply the tetrahydrate.

Why do prills and granules contain ammonium nitrate, and why does powder differ?

Pure calcium nitrate melt has a low solidification temperature (43–50 °C) and undercools, so it is very hard to form clean particles by conventional prilling or granulation; manufacturers added ammonium nitrate, at the cost of some ammonia evolution in applications where that matters.[3] In the granulation example, a hot 85–90% liquid is sprayed onto seed particles in a fluidised bed supplied with conditioned air of relative humidity below 30% at 40 °C, then cooled and screened.[4] Equipment patents exist for nitrate granulation plants.[8]

Powder differs for practical reasons. It is a finer, dustier solid with a larger surface area, so it dissolves quickly in make-up tanks but also takes up moisture faster (this is physical reasoning, not a measured comparison). Calcium nitrate starts absorbing water from air above about 46.7% relative humidity at 30 °C, lower than ammonium nitrate at 59.4%,[22] so powder needs sealed packaging and dry storage. Granules flow better and generate less dust, which suits bulk spreading; powder suits dissolving for fertigation, hydroponics or process baths. See powder vs granular calcium nitrate.

Where does the nitric acid come from, and what are the emissions?

Nitric acid is made from ammonia by the Ostwald process: ammonia is burned over a platinum–rhodium gauze at about 900 °C and pressures up to about 8 atm, and the nitrogen oxides are absorbed in water.[21] US EPA describes nitric acid production as catalytic oxidation of ammonia and requires N2O reporting from each nitric acid train.[10] So calcium nitrate is, in effect, a downstream product of ammonia, which in turn comes from Haber–Bosch synthesis.[9]

  • N2O. The ammonia burner produces nitrous oxide as a by-product. The EU BAT document gave an average of about 6 kg N2O per tonne HNO3 (about 2 t CO2-eq) for European plants of the time;[1] an industry initiative cites 6–9 kg per tonne, a global warming potential of 273 for N2O and about 95% abatement with secondary or tertiary catalysts.[17] Using the BAT average, an uncontrolled plant would be associated with roughly 4.6 kg N2O per tonne of anhydrous calcium nitrate (0.77 t HNO3 × 6 kg; illustrative arithmetic only). Modern abated plants are far lower.
  • NOx and dust. The BAT document gives NOx levels of 5–75 ppmv for new nitric acid plants, and for calcium nitrate pan granulation reports aerosol emissions normally below 4 mg nitrate-N per Nm3 of off-gas, with scrubbing and return of condensate to the process.[1]
  • CO2. The limestone route releases the carbonate carbon as CO2; the nitrophosphate route instead trades a gypsum by-product for calcium nitrate that must be sold or converted.[1][18]

When calcium nitrate is NOT the right choice

  • Do not treat the patent figures here as a plant recipe. They are published examples from specific inventions and vary by producer and product.[2][3][4]
  • Do not assume one “calcium nitrate” fits every use. Tetrahydrate, CAN-type double-salt granules and anhydrous powder differ in Ca, N, water and ammoniacal nitrogen, so a technical user and a fertiliser user need different specifications.[2]
  • Do not store or ship it as if it were non-hygroscopic. Calcium nitrate takes up water above about 46.7% relative humidity at 30 °C, and the tetrahydrate melts near 42 °C.[2][22]
  • Do not infer a supplier’s process from this page. Which route a plant uses (limestone, nitrophosphate by-product or other) changes the impurity profile; ask for the TDS and batch COA rather than assuming.[1]

Frequently asked questions

How is calcium nitrate made?

Mainly by reacting limestone or lime with nitric acid (CaCO3 + 2HNO3 → Ca(NO3)2 + CO2 + H2O), then filtering, evaporating and forming the solid. It is also recovered as a by-product of the nitrophosphate (Odda) process.[1][18]

What is the Odda process?

The Odda or nitrophosphate process digests phosphate rock in about 60–62% nitric acid instead of sulphuric acid. Cooling crystallises calcium nitrate tetrahydrate, which is filtered off; this avoids gypsum formation. The calcium nitrate is then sold as fertiliser or converted to ammonium nitrate and calcium carbonate.[1][14]

Who invented the first industrial calcium nitrate process?

Kristian Birkeland and Sam Eyde, whose electric-arc process fixed nitrogen from air. Calcium nitrate was marketed as Norgessalpeter from 1905 and the Rjukan–Notodden complex is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.[11][15][20]

Why does commercial calcium nitrate contain ammonium nitrate?

Pure calcium nitrate melt solidifies at only about 43–50 °C and undercools, so ammonium nitrate is added to permit prilling and granulation. Typical prilled material contains 6–8% ammonium nitrate.[2][3]

What is 5Ca(NO3)2·NH4NO3·10H2O?

It is the calcium ammonium nitrate decahydrate double salt that fertiliser-grade calcium nitrate (about 15.5% N, 19% Ca) closely resembles. By formula arithmetic the pure double salt is about 18.5% Ca and 15.5% N.[5][6]

What is the difference between tetrahydrate and anhydrous calcium nitrate?

The tetrahydrate holds about 30% water and melts near 42 °C; the anhydrous salt melts above 500 °C and can be made as a powder by drying at 200–300 °C in patent examples. Anhydrous material takes water back from air to form the hydrate.[2][18]

What are the environmental impacts of making calcium nitrate?

The main one is nitrous oxide from the nitric acid plant that supplies the acid, about 6 kg per tonne HNO3 on a 1990s European average and reducible by about 95% with abatement catalysts, plus NOx and nitrate aerosols from granulation, which are controlled by scrubbing.[1][17]
