Calcium nitrate for bitter pit, blossom-end rot, tipburn and cracking: spray rates, timing and what the evidence shows

Last updated: 7 October 2026  ·  Reviewed by Sujay Shrivastava - Digital Transformation Consultant
In summary

For apples and pears, extension programs recommend foliar calcium nitrate at about 3.2–5.3 lb per 100 gal (3.8–6.4 g/L) of dilute spray, applied repeatedly between petal fall and harvest: UMass/UVM suggests every two weeks, while UC ANR and Pacific Northwest guidance use at least three to five sprays about a month apart, and a rate ceiling of 5 lb/100 gal (6.0 g/L).[1][2][5] More sprays give more fruit calcium, and a 5 lb/100 gal program is credited with reducing bitter pit by 35–50% in Washington.[1][3] The evidence is not uniform: a one-year Washington State University trial on Honeycrisp found little statistical difference from untreated fruit,[24] and a 2024 review argues bitter pit is not caused by low calcium alone.[8] For tomato, pepper and watermelon blossom-end rot, universities say foliar calcium is unreliable because leaf-applied calcium does not move into the fruit, so steady irrigation comes first.[14][18]

Why do calcium-deficiency disorders happen when soil calcium is adequate?

Bitter pit is the best-known example: small pitted brown lesions in or under the apple skin that often show in storage.[7][26] Calcium reaches fruit and leaves almost entirely in the xylem (the water-conducting tissue) and is virtually immobile in the phloem. Fruit transpire far less than leaves, and fruit transpiration falls sharply after fruit set, so most calcium is delivered early in fruit growth and developing fruit can be short even when soil and leaf calcium are fine.[9] That is why bitter pit, cork spot, blossom-end rot (BER) and lettuce tipburn all appear as localised deficiencies in the tissue farthest from the transpiration stream, such as the calyx end of an apple, the blossom end of a tomato, or the inner leaves of a lettuce head.[9][19][22]

It follows that anything that favours leaves and shoots over fruit lowers fruit calcium: heavy pruning, vigorous shoot growth, excess nitrogen, and a light crop that produces large fruit.[3][5][7] Water stress and rapid swings in soil moisture limit calcium uptake and transport.[14][16] Even so, the cause is probably more than a single nutrient: a 2024 review reports that potassium and magnesium compete with calcium, that high K/Ca and (K+Mg)/Ca ratios raise bitter pit risk more reliably than calcium concentration alone, and that total fruit calcium is often a weak predictor.[8]

Where a program is stated as calcium, UMass gives 0.6–0.8 lb Ca per 100 gal (0.7–1.0 g/L) early and 0.8–1.0 lb per 100 gal (1.0–1.2 g/L) late, and notes that rates and timing can be adjusted if the yearly calcium total is kept equivalent.[1] Treat the figures as extension recommendations for dilute spraying, not as a label: always follow the product label and local advice.

How do I mix and apply a foliar calcium nitrate spray?

The steps below follow the UMass and Pacific Northwest guidance for apples and pears.[1][5][6] Calibrate the sprayer first; UMass attributes much leaf burn to inaccurate calibration, especially with concentrate spraying.[1]

  1. Pick the rate. Early season: about 3.2–4.3 lb/100 gal (3.8–5.2 g/L). After mid-July: up to 4.3–5.3 lb/100 gal (5.2–6.4 g/L), never above 5 lb/100 gal (6.0 g/L) on Anjou pears or where Pacific Northwest limits apply.[1][5][6]
  2. Fill the tank part-way with clean water and start agitation. Calcium nitrate does not raise spray pH, so no vinegar is needed (calcium chloride does).[1]
  3. Add the weighed calcium nitrate slowly and let it dissolve fully; check compatibility with other products before mixing.
  4. Add a surfactant where the program calls for it.[5]
  5. Spray to cover fruit and leaves thoroughly, because calcium does not move from leaf to fruit or between sides of the fruit.[3]
  6. Spray when drying is fast (not in cool, moist weather or in the evening) and avoid hot weather, to limit russet and burn.[3][5]
  7. Repeat on the schedule in the table above. UMass found the effect depends on the number of sprays.[1]

Does it work? What does the evidence say?

The honest answer is: usually yes in the older and Southern-Hemisphere trials, less clearly in recent Washington work.

  • Supportive: UMass reports fruit calcium rising with spray number and calls foliar sprays the most reliable and economical route.[1] Chilean Braeburn trials (1995–1999) reported about 92% control of internal bitter pit and 82% of external with calcium sprays.[10] A Stellenbosch study found calcium nitrate raised fruit calcium and cut bitter pit in Golden Delicious compared with an untreated control and a chelate.[11] A review of foliar calcium in deciduous fruit reported lower bitter pit in apple and less decay in pear, with inconsistency attributed to growth stage, weather and additives.[12]
  • Sceptical: Washington State University's Bernardita Sallato sampled two Honeycrisp sites for a year and found little difference between products and sometimes between treated and untreated fruit; calcium nitrate and calcium carbonate lowered pit incidence but not significantly, and treatments did not measurably change calcium in fruit or leaves.[24] Good Fruit Grower summarised the study under the headline that calcium treatments do little to reduce bitter pit.[25] This was a single year of work, so it is a warning, not a verdict.
  • Mechanism caveat: in the Chilean trials, subepidermal calcium explained only 53.3% of the variation in external pit,[10] and the 2024 review says total fruit calcium is a poor stand-alone predictor.[8]

Practical reading: calcium sprays are an inexpensive insurance layer for susceptible varieties and seasons, but they are not a substitute for crop load, vigour and water management.

What are the risks: russet, leaf burn and extra nitrogen?

  • Russet and fruit marking. Calcium nitrate sprays can increase russeting, especially on Golden Delicious, when drying is slow; avoid spraying in cool, moist weather or in the evening.[3] The Pacific Northwest handbook adds that higher concentrations can damage foliage and fruit and that calcium nitrate may interfere with colour.[5] UMass found no fruit injury on McIntosh but cites reports of spotting on Delicious and Golden Delicious.[1]
  • Leaf burn. UMass describes marginal leaf burn, mainly with calcium chloride, linked to calibration error.[1] Wisconsin extension advises skipping calcium chloride during prolonged dry weather to avoid residue build-up.[4]
  • Nitrogen. Calcium nitrate adds nitrogen, so UC ANR says to adjust the fertiliser program to avoid excess vigour.[2] Idaho notes that it raises nitrogen without greatly influencing tree vigour,[3] and UMass measured no rise in leaf nitrogen at the recommended dose.[1] As arithmetic: at 15.5% N, a 5 lb/100 gal spray at 300 gal/acre applies about 2.3 lb N/acre (2.6 kg/ha) per pass, so a five-spray season is about 11.6 lb N/acre (13 kg/ha).

What about blossom-end rot in tomato, pepper and watermelon?

BER is a localised calcium deficiency in the blossom end of the fruit, most often triggered by drought, heavy rain, salinity, root damage or excess ammonium nitrogen, not by low soil calcium.[14][16][17] Extension agencies are cautious about sprays: Clemson says calcium in leaves cannot move through the phloem to the fruit and the fruit skin cannot absorb it, so foliar sprays are not effective;[18] Purdue reports that calcium chloride sprays (5 lb of 78% per 100 gal, or 6.0 g/L) are reported effective in greenhouses but ineffective on field tomatoes.[14]

If a grower still sprays as an emergency measure, published calcium chloride programs are 2 tablespoons per gallon, three weekly sprays (Oregon State),[15] 4 teaspoons of 96% calcium chloride per gallon for three to four weekly sprays (Virginia Tech),[16] or 4 lb per 100 gal (4.8 g/L) four times weekly (NC State).[17] These sources specify calcium chloride, and none of the pages read gave a foliar calcium nitrate rate for BER. Their dependable advice is cultural: even soil moisture, mulch, shallow cultivation, a soil pH near 6.5 and nitrate rather than ammonium nitrogen.[14][15][16][17] Calcium nitrate is relevant mainly as a soil or fertigation calcium and nitrate source; Oregon State lists it as a moderate nitrogen option.[15] In hydroponic tomatoes, calcium nitrate concentration in the nutrient solution is adjusted per cultivar, and a Croatian rockwool study still recorded 11.6–14% affected fruit across calcium levels.[13] Pepper and watermelon are covered by the same advice; NC State gives no separate watermelon rates.[17]

Do foliar calcium sprays stop lettuce tipburn?

Tipburn is linked to low transpiration in rapidly growing inner leaves, such as fog, high humidity and head formation, not usually to low soil calcium.[19] UC IPM says foliar calcium can work if it reaches the affected tissue but is ineffective in head lettuce, and soil calcium is generally sufficient in California production areas.[19] A UC Davis survey of 15 Salinas Valley romaine fields found no correlation between tipburn, inner-leaf calcium and soil calcium, and two of the three affected fields had very low evapotranspiration in the week before harvest; calcium fertigation did not change tipburn in the one field affected.[20] Cultivar choice, steady irrigation and climate management are the main tools.

Do calcium sprays reduce cherry cracking?

Partly. Oregon State recommends six applications from pit hardening of 100 gal/acre of a 0.1–0.15% calcium solution as amino-acid or organic-acid chelates, which strengthen cell walls and lower rain cracking; calcium chloride can be applied before rain, but residues may damage leaves or dull fruit and require washing.[21] Oregon State says calcium sprays help in moderate rain, not in severe prolonged storms, and pairs them with cuticle protectants and soil moisture management.[21] For scale, 0.1–0.15% Ca equals 1.0–1.5 g/L of calcium, about 5.3–7.9 g/L of 19% calcium nitrate (calculated; the source names chelates, not calcium nitrate).

Which cultural controls matter most?

  • Water. Keep soil moisture even through early fruit growth; avoid drought followed by heavy irrigation or rain.[5][14][16]
  • Crop load. Avoid biennial bearing; a light crop gives large, low-calcium fruit.[1][7] The Pacific Northwest handbook targets 5–7 fruit per cm2 of trunk cross-section for susceptible cultivars.[5]
  • Vigour and pruning. Heavy dormant pruning and excess nitrogen push shoots that outcompete fruit for calcium.[3]
  • Potassium and magnesium. Excess K and Mg antagonise calcium uptake; the PNW handbook suggests cutting potassium 25–30% for Honeycrisp.[5][8]
  • Nitrogen form. Nitrate nitrogen rather than ammonium for BER-prone vegetables.[14][16]

When calcium nitrate is NOT the right choice

  • Do not treat foliar calcium as the fix for tomato, pepper or watermelon BER. Leaf calcium does not translocate to the fruit; correct irrigation and root-zone problems first.[14][18]
  • Head lettuce tipburn is rarely solved by spraying. Foliar calcium does not reach the inner leaves; control climate, irrigation and cultivar.[19][20]
  • Avoid spraying in hot, humid or slow-drying conditions, or above 5 lb/100 gal, because of russet, spotting and burn; use lower rates on Anjou pears.[3][5][6]
  • Do not ignore the nitrogen added on vigorous trees; count it in the nitrogen budget.[2]
  • Do not expect certainty. A single-year Washington trial on Honeycrisp found no significant benefit from any product, so check results on your own blocks.[24]

Frequently asked questions

How much calcium nitrate per gallon for bitter pit?

UC ANR gives 5 lb of 19% calcium nitrate per 100 gal (about 6.0 g/L, or 0.05 lb per gallon) for apples, at least 3 sprays monthly from May or June.[2] UMass suggests 3.2–4.3 lb/100 gal early and 4.3–5.3 lb/100 gal after mid-July, repeated every two weeks.[1]

How many calcium nitrate sprays do apples need?

Programs range from at least three monthly sprays (UC ANR) to five monthly (Pacific Northwest), at least four at two-week intervals (Ontario) and up to eight fortnightly (UMass). Fruit calcium rises with spray number.[1][2][5][22]

Does calcium nitrate spray prevent blossom-end rot?

Not reliably. Clemson says leaf calcium cannot move into fruit and fruit skin cannot absorb it,[18] and Purdue reports calcium chloride sprays ineffective on field tomatoes though effective in greenhouses.[14] Even watering and mulch are the main controls.[16][17]

Will calcium nitrate spray burn leaves or russet fruit?

It can. Idaho warns that calcium nitrate can increase russet, especially on Golden Delicious, when drying is slow,[3] and the Pacific Northwest handbook warns that high rates may damage foliage and fruit. Do not exceed 5 lb/100 gal.[5]

Do calcium sprays really work for bitter pit?

Many trials say yes: Chile reported 92% control of internal pit with calcium sprays,[10] and Washington programs cut pit 35–50%.[3] However, a one-year WSU trial on Honeycrisp found no significant difference from untreated fruit,[24] so results depend on cultivar, season and cultural management.

Calcium nitrate or calcium chloride for foliar sprays?

UMass says they are equally effective at the same actual calcium, but calcium nitrate needs 1.6 times as much product and adds nitrogen, while calcium chloride is cheaper and is more linked to leaf burn.[1]

Does tipburn in lettuce respond to calcium sprays?

Rarely in head lettuce. UC IPM says foliar calcium does not reach susceptible inner tissue, and a UC Davis field survey linked tipburn to low evapotranspiration, not soil calcium.[19][20]

Can I dip apples in calcium after harvest?

Wisconsin extension gives a 4% calcium chloride dip (33 lb per 100 gal, about 39.5 g/L) to reduce or delay bitter pit in storage,[4] though the PNW handbook rates dips less effective than field sprays.[5]
