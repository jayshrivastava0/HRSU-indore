How is calcium nitrate used to control H₂S odor and corrosion in municipal wastewater?

Last updated: 7 October 2026  ·  Reviewed by Sujay Shrivastava - Digital Transformation Consultant  ·  24 cited sources (20 high-authority)
In summary

Nitrate dosing controls hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) in sewers mainly by activating bacteria that oxidize sulfide to sulfur and sulfate, and by giving the biofilm a preferred electron acceptor so that sulfate reduction does not take over while nitrate remains.[1][3][4] Published stoichiometry is about 2.4 g of nitrate-oxygen per g of sulfide in a patent,[13] while the US EPA design manual reports that in practice roughly 10 parts nitrate per part sulfide were needed.[1] Reported field doses run from tens of mg/L nitrate on a Los Angeles force main (27 mg/L cut sulfide to about 2 mg/L) to 85 mg/L at a Canadian pump station.[1][6] Calcium nitrate and sodium nitrate have both been used; dose must be set from your own sulfide load and retention time, ideally by pilot trial.[2][13]

Why does hydrogen sulfide form in wastewater?

Raw sewage carries sulfate and organic matter. When oxygen is used up, for example in a long pressurized force main, a wet well, or a sediment layer, sulfate-reducing bacteria (SRB) in the pipe-wall biofilm use sulfate as their electron acceptor and release dissolved sulfide.[1][14] Alberta field work on pump-station force mains estimated biofilm sulfide generation at 0.08–0.15 g S/m²·h.[6] Warm temperatures, high organic load and long retention time all increase the sulfide that arrives at the next manhole or treatment plant.[1]

How does pH decide whether sulfide becomes an odor problem?

Dissolved sulfide exists as H₂S gas or as the bisulfide ion HS−, and the split depends on pH. Hydrogen sulfide is a weak acid whose gas fraction rises as pH falls.[21] The EPA design manual illustrates this: at pH 6.0, a 4.0 mg/L total dissolved sulfide is about 3.6 mg/L H₂S and 0.4 mg/L HS−.[1] Only the H₂S fraction can escape to the sewer air, so raising pH holds sulfide in solution; that is the principle behind magnesium hydroxide and caustic dosing.[2][19] Nitrate works differently and does not rely on pH.

Once H₂S reaches the pipe atmosphere it dissolves into condensate on the crown and is oxidized by sulfur-oxidizing bacteria into sulfuric acid. On concrete coupons in a Japanese manhole, the acid-producing species Acidithiobacillus thiooxidans came to make up about 70% of cells, with 3–4 mm/yr corrosion.[14] H₂S is also a worker hazard: OSHA lists a 20 ppm general-industry ceiling and 100 ppm as immediately dangerous, and notes that the sense of smell is lost at high concentrations.[9] ATSDR publishes the full toxicological profile.[10]

How does nitrate dosing actually stop H₂S?

The commonly repeated explanation, that nitrate “kills” or out-competes SRB, is only part of the story. Evidence now points to three mechanisms working together:

  1. Anoxic sulfide oxidation (the dominant effect for existing sulfide). In wastewater biofilm, nitrate decreased net sulfide production “caused by an increase in sulfide oxidation rates … rather than an inhibition of sulfate reducing bacteria,” because indigenous nitrate-reducing, sulfide-oxidizing bacteria (NR-SOB) respond within hours.[3] In a laboratory rising main, sulfide was oxidized to elemental sulfur and then to sulfate.[4]
  2. Preferred electron acceptor (prevention). Bacteria use acceptors in the order oxygen, nitrate, sulfate, so sulfate reduction is held off while nitrate is present.[1] Lab tests on non-septic wastewater showed nitrate inhibiting sulfide production until denitrification was complete.[16]
  3. Competition and inhibition effects of nitrate reduction products. A 2025 review of SRB inhibition in sewers treats chemical dosing as effective but notes drawbacks: repeated dosing, short effective duration, possible bacterial resistance, and extra load on downstream plants.[12]

The practical consequence: nitrate must be present in the water or biofilm to work. When it is consumed, sulfate reduction resumes, which is why dosing location and continuity matter.[1][8] In thick sediment, a study found nitrate did not suppress sulfide production itself; it limited accumulation by oxidation and shifted methane production deeper into the sediment.[5]

Treat the gap between about 0.7 and an EPA-style practical ratio as a planning range, not a specification. The excess over stoichiometry covers nitrate used by ordinary heterotrophs on organic matter, sulfide regeneration in biofilm, and residual that must remain for protection. Ontario’s sewage-works guidelines tell designers to use “a generous factor of safety” for side reactions and to consider pilot testing to set dosages.[2]

Retention time governs how much sulfide forms and how long nitrate is needed. Dose should scale with the hydraulic retention time of the section being protected,[6] and the Wollongong group has published on optimizing nitrate dosing strategies for sulfide and methane in sewers.[24] Recent ASCE work addresses sulfide generation in force mains and its control by nitrate.[17]

Worked example: sizing a calcium nitrate feed for one force main

This is an illustrative calculation with stated assumptions, not a design.

  • Flow: 2,000 m³/day. Sulfide to be controlled: 5 mg S/L, which is 5 g/m³, so 2,000 × 5 = 10 kg S/day.
  • Low demand case (stoichiometric, ~0.7 g NO3-N per g S): 7 kg NO3-N/day, equal to 3.5 mg NO3-N/L.
  • High demand case (EPA ~10 g nitrate per g S, taken as nitrate ion, equal to ~2.26 g NO3-N per g S): 22.6 kg NO3-N/day, or 11.3 mg NO3-N/L.
  • Product: assume a calcium nitrate powder with 14% nitrate-N (placeholder; use the nitrate-N figure from your COA, since total N includes any ammonium). Required product is about 50 kg/day (low) to 160 kg/day (high).
  • Next step: choose a starting dose in this range, trial it, and adjust against measured sulfide and residual nitrate at the far end of the main.

Combinations can lower cost: intermittent nitrate plus calcium peroxide cut chemical cost by 85% compared with continuous nitrate alone in one laboratory study,[15] and utilities such as Orange County Sanitation District report using ferrous chloride, magnesium hydroxide and calcium nitrate as their main odor-control chemicals.[23]

Calcium nitrate vs sodium nitrate vs ammonium nitrate: does the cation matter?

For sulfide chemistry the active species is the nitrate ion, and both sodium and calcium nitrate solutions are documented as successful.[13] Differences are secondary: calcium nitrate has been the subject of formal municipal trials,[7] sodium nitrate adds sodium to the stream, and ammonium nitrate adds ammonia load; the Corfu study recommended a dose of about 10 kg NH4NO3/h to balance control against ammonia increases.[16] Calcium nitrate, Ca(NO3)2, is a highly soluble salt.[22] It adds calcium (hardness) to the wastewater; this is an engineering consideration for scaling and is not covered by the sources above. Note that fertilizer-grade calcium nitrate typically contains some ammonium nitrogen, so ask for the nitrate-N and ammonium-N split.

What happens to the nitrogen, and what should be monitored?

Excess nitrate that is not consumed passes downstream as nitrate-N and enters the treatment plant, where it can be denitrified; Ontario notes nitrate produces only minor solids.[2] Over-dosing wastes chemical and raises nitrogen load; under-dosing lets sulfide return.[8] A practical monitoring set:

  • Dissolved sulfide at the end of the treated section (target set by your odor and corrosion criteria; the Nevada trial reported below 0.1 ppm).[7]
  • Gas-phase H₂S loggers at the receiving manhole or wet well; the German study used online H₂S measurement to find a daily sulfide spike pattern.[8]
  • Residual nitrate, plus pH and temperature, as measured in the Nevada trial.[7]
  • Plant effluent nitrogen where the receiving works has nitrogen limits.
  • Worker gas detection, not smell, for confined spaces.[9]

How do you run a trial?

  1. Map retention time and sulfide profile along the main (grab samples at several points, plus gas logging).[8]
  2. Calculate the dose range with the stoichiometric and practical ratios above.[1][13]
  3. Start at the mid-range, dosing near the head of the section, then test a second dosing point if sulfide returns downstream.[6][8]
  4. Adjust until end-of-section sulfide meets target while a small nitrate residual remains.
  5. Record chemical use against flow and temperature to build a dose control rule, since demand changes seasonally.[1][6]

When calcium nitrate is NOT the right choice

  • Very high sulfide loads with short retention. Iron salts or oxidants act faster than biological sulfide oxidation, and nitrate is consumed by other bacteria first.[1][2]
  • Systems with strict effluent nitrogen limits unless downstream denitrification capacity is confirmed; nitrate and ammonium both add nitrogen.[16]
  • Thick sediment or stagnant lagoons without a trial. Nitrate limited sulfide accumulation in sediment but did not stop SRB activity, and published lagoon data are limited.[5]
  • As a substitute for confined-space safety. Treatment reduces H₂S, it does not remove the need for gas detection.[9]

Frequently asked questions

How does calcium nitrate control hydrogen sulfide in sewers?

Nitrate stimulates indigenous bacteria that oxidize dissolved sulfide to sulfur and sulfate, and sulfate reduction is held off while nitrate is present. Biofilm work shows the effect comes mainly from sulfide oxidation rather than killing sulfate reducers.[1][3][4]

How much nitrate per mg of sulfide?

A patent gives 2.4 g nitrate-oxygen per g sulfide stoichiometrically (about 0.7 g NO3-N per g S by our conversion), and the EPA manual says roughly 10 parts nitrate per part sulfide were needed in practice. Pilot testing is advised.[1][2][13]

What nitrate concentration is dosed in force mains?

Reported examples: 27 mg/L nitrate cut a Los Angeles force main discharge to about 2 mg/L sulfide, and 50 mg/L or more gave about 1 mg/L; an Alberta pump station used 85 mg/L continuously.[1][6]

Is calcium nitrate better than ferric chloride for sewer odor?

Not universally. Iron precipitates sulfide quickly and is often cheaper but adds sludge; nitrate is non-hazardous and low-solids and also prevents new sulfide. One utility paper found a low-hazard iron product outperformed calcium nitrate on cost.[2][20]

Does calcium nitrate work as well as sodium nitrate?

Both solutions have been used successfully for dissolved sulfide, since the nitrate ion does the work. Ammonium nitrate adds ammonia load.[13][16]

Why does sulfide come back after dosing?

Nitrate is consumed as water travels. In a 9 km German pressure sewer, sulfide regenerated after about 3 hours of residence in the dosed section, so dosing location and continuity matter.[8]

Does nitrate dosing create a nitrogen problem?

It adds nitrate-N (and ammonium-N if ammonium nitrate is used) to the stream, so downstream plants with nitrogen limits should be consulted; ammonium nitrate dosing was limited in Corfu for ammonia reasons.[16]
