Calcium nitrate as a deicing agent, and how it compares with every other common deicer

Last updated: 7 October 2026  ·  Reviewed by Sujay Shrivastava - Digital Transformation Consultant
In summary

Calcium nitrate is a niche, chloride-free deicer, not a mainstream one. Russian laboratory work (Kurnakov Institute) mapped the Ca(NO3)2–water freezing behaviour from 0 to −31 °C and reported no attack on metals, aircraft alloys or cement concrete, recommending anhydrous granular calcium nitrate for runway pavements.[2] The mainstream deicers are still sodium chloride (eutectic −21 °C), magnesium chloride (−33 °C), calcium chloride (−51 °C), calcium magnesium acetate (−27.5 °C) and potassium acetate (−60 °C),[1] plus glycols for aircraft.[11] The honest drawback of calcium nitrate is nitrogen: it adds nitrate to runoff, and deicer patents and extension guidance treat nitrogen salts as environmentally undesirable.[14][16] It is not named in the FAA or Transport Canada runway-chemical specifications we reviewed.[7][9]

Is calcium nitrate a deicer?

Yes, but a specialist one. Any soluble salt lowers the freezing point of water, and deicers work by forming a brine on the ice surface.[16] Calcium nitrate is highly water-soluble,[24] so it qualifies on chemistry. The published evidence is thin and concentrated in one research group: the Kurnakov Institute of General and Inorganic Chemistry (Russian Academy of Sciences), which studied the Ca(NO3)2–water system from 0 to −31 °C, built the ice-crystallisation curve (polytherm), calculated the ice-melting capacity under equilibrium conditions, and measured corrosion of metals, alloys and cement concrete.[2] The same group also described deicers based on magnesium and calcium nitrate mixtures, including a liquid solution and a solid dehydrated composition, and noted that the liquid form needs a corrosion inhibitor to bring metal corrosion down.[3]

We found no calcium nitrate entry in the Transport Canada runway ice-control advisory circular (which points to SAE AMS 1431 and AMS 1435 products)[7] or in the Wikipedia overview of road and runway deicers.[23] Treat it as a niche or emerging option, not a standard purchase specification.

What are the common deicing agents and products?

Deicing chemicals fall into a few families: chlorides (sodium, calcium, magnesium), acetates and formates, urea, glycols for aircraft, agricultural by-products such as beet juice, and a small group of nitrates.[16][17][23] The table below compares them. Eutectic values are the theoretical lowest temperature of a brine at its best concentration;[1] real-world “lowest practical working temperature” is always warmer, which is why the Ontario conservation-authority brief lists NaCl at −9 °C although its eutectic is −21 °C.[1][17]

How cold can each deicer work?

The FHWA anti-icing manual lists eutectics of −21 °C for NaCl, −33 °C for MgCl2, −51 °C for CaCl2, −27.5 °C for CMA and −60 °C for potassium acetate.[1] The same manual advises making sodium chloride anti-icing brine “as close to 23.3 percent as possible, but no more than 25 percent”.[1] The Ontario brief gives lower practical limits (NaCl −9 °C, MgCl2 −15 °C, CaCl2 −29 °C, CMA −7 °C).[17] For calcium nitrate, the Russian study states that the minimum effective temperature was determined experimentally but the abstract available to us does not give the number, so we do not quote one; the equilibrium was measured down to −31 °C.[2]

Is calcium nitrate corrosive or damaging to concrete and aircraft?

In the Kurnakov study, calcium nitrate solutions did not attack metals, aircraft alloys or cement concrete, and that is the main reason the authors recommend it for runways.[2] Compare chlorides: magnesium and calcium chloride react with cement paste, with calcium oxychloride forming at concentrations typically above 15% by mass,[4] and the type of deicing salt strongly changes the type and extent of chemical damage in slag-cement pastes.[5] Magnesium chloride can be more corrosive than sodium chloride to automobile components in humid conditions, while sodium chloride is worse under immersion and arid conditions.[6] This is a single laboratory study of calcium nitrate, so a road or airport authority would need its own corrosion and concrete testing before use. Airfield chemicals are also tested for carbon-brake oxidation and cadmium-plate corrosion under SAE AMS 1431 and 1435.[7]

What is the environmental problem with nitrate deicers?

Chlorides do not degrade; once in water they persist and can mobilise metals and alter soil structure and ecosystems.[13] EPA freshwater criteria for chloride are 230 mg/L chronic and 860 mg/L acute.[19] Calcium nitrate avoids chloride, which is its attraction. It substitutes a different load. Extension guidance lists nitrogen salts such as urea and ammonium sulfate as rarely used because nitrogen runoff harms groundwater,[16] and a 1994 deicer patent described the inclusion of nitrates in a prior-art formulation as potentially damaging to the environment.[14] Nitrate has an enforceable U.S. drinking-water limit of 10 mg/L (as nitrogen).[18] EPA’s airport deicing rule specifically targets urea (through ammonia and oxygen demand), not nitrate,[10][12] but the same nitrogen-in-runoff logic applies near surface water and wells. The USGS has published work on the aquatic toxicity of airfield pavement deicer materials,[21] and any site using calcium nitrate should treat runoff discharge as a design issue.

Where does calcium nitrate honestly fit?

  • Possible fit: chloride-sensitive sites where corrosion of metal or concrete matters and runoff is controlled or small, such as aircraft-adjacent pavements (the use the research targets),[2] or small walkway areas using a zeolite-based anti-slip blend.[22]
  • Not a replacement for road salt. Rock salt is the cheapest and most widely used deicer[16][17] and no evidence was found that calcium nitrate competes with it on price or at highway scale.
  • Not an approved runway product by default. FAA and Transport Canada guidance points airports to SAE AMS 1431 (solids) and AMS 1435 (fluids) products,[7][8] and the circular we read does not list calcium nitrate.[7]
  • Evidence level: one research group, laboratory scale, published in 2019.[2] It has not been validated here by an independent field trial.

What about zeolite and calcium nitrate anti-slip deicers?

Some retailers sell a solid anti-slip agent made of zeolite containing calcium nitrate, described as chloride-free and used between 0 and −15 °C at 5–20 dkg/m2 (50–200 g/m2), with the manufacturer advising dosage trials.[22] These are manufacturer claims, not independent test data, and we cite the product only as an example that the combination exists commercially. Do not treat the rate as a specification.

How do aircraft and runway deicing differ from road deicing?

Aircraft are deiced with glycol fluids: Type I (deicing) and Type IV (thickened anti-icing), mostly propylene glycol with 1–3% additives,[11] classified under SAE specifications.[25] Pavements use solid or liquid chemicals meeting AMS 1431 or 1435, and chlorides are generally avoided on airfields because of aircraft corrosion risk.[7][23] Potassium acetate dominates U.S. pavement use, at 22,538 tons per year in EPA’s survey, followed by urea (4,127), propylene glycol fluids (3,883), sodium acetate (3,100) and sodium formate (1,117).[11] Glycols exert a very high oxygen demand when they degrade, which is the central regulatory concern for aircraft deicing runoff.[11]

What should a buyer specify if testing calcium nitrate as a deicer?

  1. Define the pavement type (concrete, asphalt, bridge deck, airfield) and the lowest temperature you must handle; compare against the eutectics above.[1]
  2. Ask the supplier for form (powder, granular, anhydrous or hydrated), moisture, calcium and nitrogen content, and insoluble residue, with a batch Certificate of Analysis. The research recommended anhydrous granular for runways,[2] so powder trials may behave differently.
  3. Run your own corrosion and concrete-scaling tests; do not rely on one published study.[2][4]
  4. Check local rules on nitrogen in stormwater and groundwater before use.[10][18]
  5. Use the lowest effective quantity; extension guidance advises using deicers sparingly to limit soil, water and concrete damage.[16]

When calcium nitrate is NOT the right choice

  • Do not use it near wells, streams or lakes without runoff control. Calcium nitrate adds nitrate nitrogen; the U.S. drinking-water limit is 10 mg/L as nitrogen.[18]
  • Do not assume it is an approved airfield deicer. FAA and Transport Canada point to SAE AMS 1431 and 1435 products, and the circular we read does not name calcium nitrate.[7][8]
  • Do not buy it expecting a cheap road-salt replacement. No price or large-scale field data were found, while rock salt remains the lowest-cost option.[17]
  • Do not rely on a single lab study for corrosion claims. The “no attack on metals, alloys and concrete” finding comes from one research group.[2]
  • Do not use it on vegetation-sensitive land without a trial: deicers of every type can accumulate in soil and harm plants,[16] and calcium nitrate also supplies nitrogen.

Frequently asked questions

Is calcium nitrate used as a deicer?

It is a niche deicer. Russian laboratory research studied it from 0 to −31 °C and reported no attack on metals, aircraft alloys or cement concrete, but it is not named in the Transport Canada runway ice-control circular and is far less used than salt, magnesium chloride or acetates.[2][7]

What are the main deicing agents?

Sodium chloride, calcium chloride, magnesium chloride, calcium magnesium acetate and potassium acetate for roads and pavements; urea and organic salts (sodium acetate, sodium formate) for airfield pavements; and propylene or ethylene glycol fluids for aircraft.[1][11][16]

Which deicer works at the lowest temperature?

By eutectic temperature, potassium acetate (−60 °C) and calcium chloride (−51 °C) lead, followed by magnesium chloride (−33 °C), CMA (−27.5 °C) and sodium chloride (−21 °C). Practical working limits are warmer.[1][17]

Is calcium nitrate better than rock salt for concrete?

A single laboratory study found calcium nitrate does not attack cement concrete,[2] whereas chloride deicers can damage concrete, with calcium and magnesium chloride forming harmful oxychloride phases at high concentration.[4] Independent field data are lacking, so test before adopting.

What is the environmental downside of calcium nitrate deicing?

It adds nitrate nitrogen to runoff. Extension guidance says nitrogen-based deicers are rarely used because of groundwater impact, and the EPA drinking-water limit for nitrate is 10 mg/L as nitrogen.[16][18]

What deicer is used on airport runways?

U.S. airports mostly use potassium acetate (about 80% of airfield deicing chemical use in EPA’s survey), with sodium acetate, sodium formate and some urea; large airports must use non-urea deicers or meet an ammonia limit.[10][11][12]

Is there a zeolite and calcium nitrate deicer?

Yes. Retailers sell a zeolite-based anti-slip product containing calcium nitrate, rated by the vendor for 0 to −15 °C; these are manufacturer claims, not independent test results.[22]
