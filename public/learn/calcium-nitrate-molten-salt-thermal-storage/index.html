Calcium nitrate in molten salt: heat transfer and thermal storage for concentrated solar power

Last updated: 7 October 2026  ·  Reviewed by Sujay Shrivastava - Digital Transformation Consultant  ·  21 cited sources (18 high-authority)
In summary

Calcium nitrate is used as a minority component of molten nitrate salt mixtures for concentrated solar power (CSP) heat transfer and thermal storage. Mixed with sodium and potassium nitrate it forms a ternary salt that melts at about 133 °C, and adding lithium nitrate gives quaternary fluids with liquidus below 100 °C and stability above 500 °C.[1][2] The trade-off is documented: calcium nitrate is the lowest-cost nitrate constituent and lowers the melting point, but it raises viscosity, promotes glassy melts near freezing and is the least favoured constituent for thermal stability.[1] It is a research and specialty use, not a commodity one: today’s commercial towers mostly use binary sodium–potassium “solar salt”.[5][6]

Why molten salt, and why nitrates

Concentrated solar power plants focus sunlight to heat a fluid, then store that heat so electricity can be generated after sunset. Molten nitrate salts are the proven storage medium: in a direct two-tank molten-salt power tower the same salt collects heat in the receiver and is stored in a hot tank, then passed through a steam generator.[5][6][17] For background on thermal energy storage and tower designs see [19][20] Recent towers such as Crescent Dunes in Nevada were built with about 10 hours of storage.[7] Nitrate salts are attractive because they are liquid at moderate temperatures, have very low vapour pressure, are non-flammable and are far cheaper than synthetic heat-transfer oil.[1][2]

Where calcium nitrate fits

The standard binary salt of sodium and potassium nitrate has a relatively high freezing point, which means a plant must spend energy and care to keep every pipe above it. Researchers therefore look for ternary and quaternary blends with lower melting points. Calcium nitrate is one of the cheapest additives that lowers the melting point: a Ca(NO3)2–NaNO3–KNO3 mixture melts at about 133 °C,[1] and optimisation studies of the ternary system map its phase diagram and thermophysical properties.[9][10][11] A Sandia-developed quaternary fluid combining sodium, potassium, lithium and calcium nitrates reaches a liquidus below 100 °C and stays stable above 500 °C.[2][3] A European patent also covers calcium potassium nitrate salt for heat transfer fluids.[12]

Cost: why calcium nitrate is attractive

In the Sandia study, the expected cost ranking of the constituents by cation was calcium < sodium < potassium < lithium. A study quoted there estimated binary solar salt at about 0.50 USD/kg and a calcium-containing ternary at about 0.90 USD/kg, versus roughly 3–4 USD/kg for organic heat-transfer oils.[1] These are 2008-era estimates; use current quotes for any real project.

The engineering catch: viscosity, stability and corrosion

  • Viscosity. Calcium nitrate additions significantly increase viscosity, and calcium-containing melts tend to form glassy, viscous liquids near the freezing temperature, with about 200 cP near the liquidus in the quaternary tested.[1][11]
  • Thermal stability. Sandia concluded that calcium nitrate is the least favoured constituent for thermal stability, while lithium nitrate has only a modest effect on maximum usable temperature.[1] Stability of nitrate/nitrite salts also depends strongly on cover-gas atmosphere and pre-heating history, and thermodynamic property data for nitrate melts is published openly.[13][14][15][16]
  • Corrosion. Nitrate melts decompose to nitrite and oxygen, and the resulting oxide ions are far more corrosive than nitrate itself; corrosion resistance of Fe–Cr–Ni alloys deteriorates sharply at higher temperatures.[4] Corrosion-resistant coatings are an active research area.[8]

What this means for a buyer of calcium nitrate

  1. Thermal-storage salts are blended from high-purity, low-water feedstock; the lab work cited here started from reagent-grade Ca(NO3)2 tetrahydrate that was dehydrated by slow heating.[1]
  2. Chloride, sulphate, magnesium and insolubles matter because they change corrosion behaviour and melt chemistry — set limits in writing before quoting.[4]
  3. Plan on pilot-scale melting and thermal-cycling tests; the literature itself says further development is needed to tune proportions.[1][10]
  4. Volumes are project-based and lumpy; this is a specialty, not a spot-market, use.[5]

When calcium nitrate is NOT the right choice

  • Commercial CSP tank fills today. Operating molten-salt towers use binary sodium–potassium solar salt, not calcium-containing blends.[5][6]
  • High-temperature (>550 °C) service — nitrate decomposition and corrosion accelerate and calcium nitrate is the least stable constituent.[1][4]
  • Anyone expecting a ready-to-melt grade from a fertilizer product. Fertilizer-grade calcium nitrate carries water of hydration and has not been qualified for thermal-storage use.[1]

Frequently asked questions

Is calcium nitrate used in molten salt for solar power?

Yes, as a minority component in ternary (Ca–Na–K) and quaternary (Na–K–Li–Ca) nitrate fluids studied for concentrated solar power. Commercial towers mostly use binary sodium–potassium solar salt.[1][2][5]

What is the melting point of a calcium nitrate molten salt mixture?

A Ca(NO3)2–NaNO3–KNO3 mixture melts at about 133 °C; a Sandia quaternary with lithium nitrate has a liquidus below 100 °C.[1][2]

Why add calcium nitrate to molten salt?

It lowers the melting point and is the lowest-cost nitrate constituent, which reduces the energy needed to keep the salt molten and the cost of the fluid inventory.[1]

What are the downsides of calcium nitrate in molten salt?

Higher viscosity, a tendency to form glassy melts near freezing, and the least favourable effect on thermal stability among the constituents studied.[1][11]

How much heat storage do molten-salt towers provide?

Recent molten-salt power towers such as Crescent Dunes were designed for about 10 hours of storage.[7]

Can I use fertilizer-grade calcium nitrate for molten salt?

Not without qualification. The research uses dehydrated, high-purity feedstock, and you would need to set and verify moisture, chloride and trace-metal limits first.[1]
