Calcium nitrate for lake sediment remediation: how nitrate injection works, what doses were used and where it failed

Last updated: 7 October 2026  ·  Reviewed by Sujay Shrivastava - Digital Transformation Consultant  ·  25 cited sources (21 high-authority)
In summary

Calcium nitrate is used in lakes as a sediment oxidant: injected 15–20 cm into organic sediment, its nitrate is consumed by denitrifying bacteria in place of oxygen, which oxidises organic matter, suppresses sulfide and helps iron oxides bind phosphorus (the Riplox method).[1][4] Published doses range from about 24 g N/m² (White Lough, judged too low) through 105 g N/m² (Long Lake, Minnesota) to 141 g N/m² (Lake Lillesjön, Sweden, the best-known success, combined with ferric chloride and lime); a 2021 laboratory study recommended 45.3–141 g N/m².[1][7] The evidence is mixed and rests on few whole-lake cases: Lillesjön worked, Trekanten and Long Lake did not, usually because iron was scarce or external phosphorus kept arriving.[1][3] Nitrate, nitrite and ammonium release is the main risk, and alum, lanthanum-modified clay, oxygenation and dredging are better-documented alternatives in many lakes.[8][9][10]

What problem does calcium nitrate solve in a lake?

In a eutrophic lake, organic sediment built up over years consumes oxygen. When oxygen is gone, bacteria switch to sulfate, producing sulfide (the rotten-egg odour), and iron(III) oxides that held phosphate are reduced to iron(II) and release it. That sediment-sourced phosphorus is the internal load that keeps algal blooms going even after external sources are cut.[1][25] Nitrate is a stronger electron acceptor than sulfate, so supplying it in the sediment lets denitrifiers oxidise organic matter and keeps the iron oxidised.[1][5]

How does the Riplox method work?

Ripl reported in 1976 that injecting nitrate into sapropelic (organic, oxygen-free) sediment induced vigorous denitrification, degrading organic matter, oxidising iron compounds and inactivating phosphorus by sorption to the oxidised iron.[4][5] Nitrate reduction also converts iron sulfides to ferric iron and elemental sulfur, so the ferric hydroxide that co-precipitates phosphate can form.[1] The effect is reversible: if nitrate and oxygen are exhausted again, sulfate reduction resumes and phosphate is re-released.[1] This is why long-term success depends on cutting the external load.[1][9]

How much calcium nitrate is applied per m²?

Doses are reported in grams of nitrate nitrogen per square metre of sediment surface, not as product weight. Published values are below; the conversions to product are our arithmetic, assuming fertilizer-type calcium nitrate at 15.5% N (about 6.5 g product per g N).

For scale, Lillesjön is 4.2 ha, so 141 g N/m² over the whole lake is roughly 5.9 t of nitrogen, or about 38 t of a 15.5% N product (illustrative arithmetic). Dose is site-specific: Ripl analysed each sediment for a broad range of parameters to choose chemicals and doses,[1] and a patent on in-situ sediment deodorising describes calculating the dose from the sediment’s acid-volatile sulfide and organic carbon content (a patent example, not a standard).[23] We found no reliable published dose “per kg of organic matter” that applies across lakes; use per-m² and per-sediment-volume values from a core test.

Which lakes responded, and which did not?

  • Lillesjön, Sweden: success. Calcium nitrate, ferric chloride and lime were injected 15–20 cm deep; hypolimnetic phosphorus and BOD dropped and nitrate vanished within weeks.[1] A university-hosted introduction reports that in Ripl’s whole-lake experiment (lake not named there), sediment oxygen uptake was unchanged and phosphorus remained much lower nine years after nitrate treatment.[5]
  • Trekanten, Sweden: failure. Calcium nitrate alone (13.4 ha, 3.9 m mean depth) gave “no significant, permanent improvement” in hypolimnetic oxygen and phosphorus.[3] A review attributes it to low iron in the sediment; adding ferric chloride and lime may have helped.[1]
  • Long Lake, Minnesota: short-lived. Initial phosphorus reduction, then return to pre-treatment conditions, blamed on external loading.[1]
  • White Lough: partial. About 25% of the added calcium and 15% of the nitrate escaped to the water column straight after treatment.[1]
  • Schlei (Germany): proposal. Ripl proposed using nitrate-rich treated effluent instead of injection; enclosure tests gave 0.42–0.80 g N/m²/day denitrification at 5–15 mg NO3-N/L.[1]

Honest summary: the review found denitrification occurred as predicted in every case, but outcomes were “mixed,” and the whole-lake record is only a handful of 1970s–80s projects.[1] Newer work is mostly laboratory and microcosm scale (for example, 75% and 89% phosphorus reductions in water and pore water in tropical reservoir sediment microcosms).[6] We did not find verified Netherlands or other German whole-lake dosing records to cite.

What does it do to sulfide and odour?

Because nitrate reduction consumes acid-volatile sulfide, calcium nitrate is also studied for black, odorous sediment. In the Brazilian reservoir microcosms, denitrification consumed nearly all added nitrate and oxidised acid-volatile sulfide.[6] A 2021 study found nitrate turned surface sediment more oxidised and removed mobile Fe, S, P and As from the top ~10 cm, with a temporary dissolved-iron rise from iron sulfide dissolution.[7]

Is it safe? Risks, side effects and regulation

  • Nitrate, nitrite and ammonium release. In-situ injection releases nitrate, intermediate nitrite and sediment ammonium; ammonium poses the greatest ecological risk, especially in stagnant water and ammonia-rich sediment.[8] A separate study titled for the purpose examines the sharp ammonium increase and proposes subsurface injection as an alternative.[21]
  • Toxicity to test organisms. Early toxicity to Ceriodaphnia silvestrii and Chironomus xanthus from high nitrate/nitrite was seen in microcosms but dissipated by the end.[6]
  • Benchmarks. The US EPA freshwater ammonia criteria are 17 mg TAN/L (acute) and 1.9 mg TAN/L (chronic) at pH 7 and 20 °C;[12] WHO’s drinking-water guideline for nitrate is 50 mg/L as nitrate, set to protect bottle-fed infants from methaemoglobinaemia, relevant if the lake is a drinking source.[13]
  • pH. The pH issue in Riplox comes mainly from ferric chloride, whose dissolution creates acidity; Lillesjön used slaked lime to offset it.[1] In shallow, high-pH, warm lakes, iron redox may not control phosphate release, so nitrate may not inactivate phosphorus there.[1]
  • Adding nitrogen. Some limnologists argue phosphorus control alone is the key to managing cyanobacteria and that nitrogen reduction adds cost without benefit;[17] the risk of nitrogen additions is therefore ecological side effects and regulatory acceptance, not whether it fuels blooms, and evidence is debated.
  • Regulation. A 1988 vendor listing noted no restrictions on Riplox at that time;[1] current permit rules vary by country, so confirm with the water authority before any in-lake application.
  • Mitigation research. Studies combine calcium nitrate with anion-exchange resin capping, and compare calcium nitrate with Phoslock, to limit nitrate release and compare performance.[20][22]

In that Dutch trial the researchers judged lanthanum-modified bentonite the best balance of effectiveness, practicality, cost and environment for that pond; they stress site-specific factors.[10] Calcium nitrate’s niche is organic, sulfidic sediments with enough iron where oxygen demand and odour matter as much as phosphorus.

Can calcium nitrate be used for aquaculture pond bottoms?

The sourced evidence is thin. Aquaculture literature says nitrate in pond water supports denitrification and helps prevent low redox at the sediment–water interface, and that where bottoms cannot be dried, nitrate fertilizer can be applied as a soil oxidant.[18] That source does not name calcium nitrate, and sediment denitrification in fish ponds is itself still being characterised.[19] We found no verified calcium nitrate dose for pond bottoms. Because nitrite and ammonia are toxic to cultured animals, any trial should be small, monitored and designed by an aquaculture specialist.

How would a lake or pond team plan a calcium nitrate trial?

  1. Measure sediment organic matter, acid-volatile sulfide, iron and phosphorus fractions in cores.[1][23]
  2. Quantify and control external phosphorus loads first.[9]
  3. Run core incubations with a dose series (White Lough used 6.2–61.7 g N/m²).[1]
  4. Decide whether ferric chloride and lime are needed (Lillesjön) or nitrate alone is enough.[1]
  5. Inject below the sediment surface (about 15–20 cm) and monitor nitrate, nitrite, ammonium, sulfide, phosphorus and oxygen for at least a full stratified season.[1][8]

When calcium nitrate is NOT the right choice

  • Lakes with strong, continuing external phosphorus loading. Long Lake’s benefit faded, and alum longevity also falls when external loads stay high.[1][9]
  • Iron-poor sediment treated with nitrate alone. Trekanten showed no lasting improvement; test iron first.[1][3]
  • Ammonia-rich, stagnant or drinking-water-source lakes where nitrite and ammonium release may exceed toxicity benchmarks.[8][12][13]
  • Shallow, warm, high-pH lakes where iron redox may not control phosphate release.[1]
  • As an uncontrolled “pour-in” or aquaculture treatment without a specialist-designed dose, trial and permit check.[8][18]

Frequently asked questions

What is the Riplox method?

Riplox is a lake restoration method developed by W. Ripl in Sweden in which calcium nitrate (sometimes with ferric chloride and lime) is injected into organic sediment so denitrification oxidises organic matter and iron binds phosphorus.[1][4]

How much calcium nitrate is used to treat lake sediment per square metre?

Published doses range from 23.7 g N/m² at White Lough to 105 g N/m² at Long Lake and 141 g N/m² at Lillesjön; a 2021 lab study recommended 45.3–141 g N/m². Dose is site-specific and best set by a core test.[1][7]

Does calcium nitrate really reduce internal phosphorus load?

Sometimes. It worked at Lillesjön and in microcosms (75% and 89% reductions in water and pore water), but failed or faded at Trekanten and Long Lake, so evidence is mixed.[1][6]

Can calcium nitrate remove sulfide and odour from sediment?

In laboratory studies nitrate oxidised acid-volatile sulfide and removed mobile sulfur and iron from the top ~10 cm, but a temporary dissolved-iron rise occurred.[6][7]

Is calcium nitrate sediment treatment harmful to fish and invertebrates?

Nitrite and especially ammonium release are the main concerns; one microcosm study saw early toxicity to test invertebrates that later dissipated. Compare ammonia against the US EPA criteria of 17 mg TAN/L acute and 1.9 mg TAN/L chronic.[6][8][12]

Is calcium nitrate better than alum or Phoslock for lakes?

Not generally. Alum has stronger multi-lake evidence (longevity 7 to over 47 years in six Swedish lakes) and a Dutch trial favoured lanthanum-modified bentonite for one pond; calcium nitrate suits iron-containing organic sediments where oxidation and odour matter.[9][10]

Can I use calcium nitrate to treat my fish or shrimp pond bottom?

Aquaculture sources describe nitrate as a soil oxidant, but they do not name calcium nitrate or give a verified dose. Use a monitored small trial with specialist advice.[18][19]
