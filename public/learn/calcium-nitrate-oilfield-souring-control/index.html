Calcium nitrate for oilfield souring and sulfide control: how nitrate injection works, what doses are reported, and where it fails

Last updated: 7 October 2026  ·  Reviewed by Sujay Shrivastava - Digital Transformation Consultant  ·  24 cited sources (18 high-authority)
In summary

Nitrate injection controls reservoir souring, the microbial production of H2S in waterflooded oil fields, by stimulating nitrate-reducing bacteria that compete with sulfate-reducing bacteria (SRB) for degradable organics and that oxidise sulfide directly.[1] The nitrite they produce is a strong SRB inhibitor.[4] Calcium nitrate has documented North Sea field use: on the Gullfaks field it replaced 240,000 litres per year of biocide and had run for more than three years by 2005.[21] Reported doses are system-specific: a patent background cites roughly 25–500 ppm nitrate plus nitrite in injection water,[13] lab bioreactors needed 4–10 mM nitrate (about 250–620 mg/L),[1][4] and field failures are documented when the oil, salinity or nitrogen supply is wrong.[3][7] For biogas digesters, calcium nitrate dosing is reported to cut H2S, but the evidence is thin and partly vendor-linked.[19]

What is reservoir souring and why does nitrate stop it?

Seawater and many produced waters carry sulfate. Once injected into a warm, organic-rich reservoir, sulfate-reducing bacteria turn it into sulfide, souring the oil, water and gas, corroding steel and raising treating costs.[5][23] Nitrate treatment works through three overlapping routes: biocompetitive exclusion (heterotrophic nitrate reducers use the same organic electron donors as SRB), direct sulfide oxidation by NR-SOB, and nitrite inhibition of SRB.[1][4] A 2019 modelling study concluded that nitrite inhibition was the major mechanism whether nitrate reduction was autotrophic or heterotrophic, and that the nitrite-to-nitrate reduction-rate ratio can indicate how well a treatment will work.[10] Field data suggest the dominant nitrate pathway varies: in one mesothermic field, ammonium in produced water did not rise under nitrate injection, pointing to denitrification rather than reduction to ammonium.[6]

This page covers oilfield and biogas use. For municipal sewers, force mains and lift stations, see calcium nitrate for H₂S odour control in wastewater; the biology is shared but the dosing points, targets and risks differ.

Where is nitrate used in oil and gas?

  • Waterflood injection water. Continuous low-level dosing into seawater or produced-water injection. A patent description notes that calcium, magnesium or potassium nitrate can be added during the main waterflood phase for souring control, and that calcium nitrate can double as a fines stabiliser during well commissioning.[14]
  • Reservoir treatment of an already soured field. Nitrate and/or nitrite solutions injected into an injection well in contact with a producer to cut sulfide in production fluids.[13]
  • Produced-water storage ponds. A 2025 trial added nitrate directly to a high-salinity storage pond in a hydraulic-fracturing setting; sulfate stayed stable over 4 weeks while the SRB share fell and nitrate and nitrite fluctuated after dosing; the study reports no measured sulfide in the pond.[9]
  • Topsides and produced-water reinjection. Here the main question is corrosion rather than souring (see risks below).[8]

What case studies exist for calcium nitrate?

The best-known is the North Sea. Statoil used calcium nitrate on Gullfaks, where it replaced an annual 240,000 litres of biocide; the company reported more than three years of operation by December 2005, with the same approach on Veslefrikk (replacing biocides to reduce pipe corrosion) and Statfjord.[21] In Canada, the Medicine Hat Glauconitic C heavy-oil field is injected with nitrate and has served as a research field for how oil composition controls nitrate use.[3][4] A Saskatchewan Bakken shale-oil study found that nitrate injection can prevent souring if salinity stays high, suggesting nitrate-amended reinjection of saline produced water may be cost effective.[2] Publicly available field dose records are sparse, so most numbers below come from bioreactors, patents and modelling.

Conversion note: 1 mM nitrate is about 62 mg/L as NO3−. A single “mg nitrate per mg sulfide” ratio is not a reliable design figure from these sources: the demand depends on the organics, the oil (toluene soaks up nitrate),[3] the nitrate pathway, and whether the aim is prevention or removal of existing sulfide. Nitrate demand is better set by a treatability or bioreactor test on the actual water and oil, then tuned by monitoring. A common field failure is under-dosing: too little nitrate can leave SRB growing.[22]

Is calcium nitrate better than sodium or ammonium nitrate?

The sources here do not contain a head-to-head field trial of nitrate salts, so any ranking would be speculation. What can be said:

  • Calcium nitrate has North Sea field precedent[21] and is named in a patent among injection-water salts that serve souring control and fines stabilisation.[14] It is also the salt in the digester work discussed below.[19]
  • Ammonium nitrate was used for sewer sulfide control in Corfu, where the authors judged the added ammonia load tolerable at about 10 kg/h.[16] In reservoirs the extra ammonium is a nitrogen source for microbes; in a nitrogen-limited column, nitrate addition even stimulated SRB-containing groups.[7]
  • Calcium as a cation is a check item in sulfate-rich water, where calcium sulfate scaling is a general oilfield concern. This is basic water chemistry, not tested in the cited sources, so run a scaling check on the mixed water.
  • Handling: calcium nitrate dissolves readily and is supplied as a concentrate; a patent notes a concentrate tank may sit at the injection site.[14][24]

What are the risks and how does nitrate treatment fail?

  • Corrosion and pitting. In microcosms of produced fluids from two FPSOs held at 54 °C, untreated live replicates had the highest general corrosion (up to 0.48 mm/year), nitrate- or nitrite-treated fluids were below 0.12 mm/year, but nitrite breakthrough showed the highest pitting density.[8] Laboratory and field results on corrosion are not uniform.
  • Nitrate consumed by the oil, not the sulfide. Success is inversely related to toluene content, because nitrate reduced on toluene is not available to suppress SRB.[3]
  • Salinity and temperature. High salinity favoured nitrite accumulation and SRB inhibition in Bakken cultures; at low salinity nitrite did not accumulate.[2]
  • Nitrogen supply and electron-donor excess. Where organic acids exceeded nitrate demand and nitrogen was limiting, 25 mg/L nitrate did not control souring.[7]
  • Mixed systems. Different platforms respond differently: one FPSO responded as expected while nitrate was less effective on the other.[8] Treat each system separately.
  • Nitrogen species in produced water. Monitor nitrate, nitrite and ammonium: in one mesothermic field, ammonium in producing wells fell on average from 0.3 to 0.2 mM over 2 years of nitrate injection, but that is one field and may not transfer.[6]

Does calcium nitrate work in biogas digesters and industrial effluents?

The same biology applies in anaerobic digesters: denitrifiers outcompete SRB for substrate and nitrite inhibits sulfate reduction. Proceedings by authors from the University of Kaiserslautern and Yara Industrial report that calcium nitrate dosing gave higher methane yield, lower H2S, substrate savings, better viscosity and a more robust plant, with enhanced denitrification and methanogenesis and less sulfate reduction.[19] A separate WEF proceedings abstract describes a sludge-digestion process with a nitrate recycle loop (not calcium nitrate dosing) in which pilot H2S in biogas was consistently cut by at least 90% without ferric chloride, which shows the nitrate route works at pilot scale.[20] Treat this as promising but limited and partly vendor-linked evidence: a 2021 critical review of digester H2S management centred on iron salts, microaeration and post-treatment rather than nitrate.[15] For industrial effluents the closest published evidence is from sewers, where nitrate dosing of sewer biofilms is reported to reduce methane formation as well as sulfide,[17] and where nitrite-derived free nitrous acid has been shown to damage the SRB Desulfovibrio vulgaris in lab work.[18] Trial on your own effluent before committing.

How is a nitrate souring-control programme run?

  1. Characterise the water and oil. Sulfate, organic acids (VFA), temperature, salinity, toluene or alkylbenzene content, existing sulfide.[3][4]
  2. Test in a bioreactor or batch microcosm with field water and oil; record nitrate required to stop sulfide.[1][4]
  3. Choose a dosing strategy. Continuous low-level injection is the common approach; the patent route for existing souring injects nitrate/nitrite solution at an injector.[13]
  4. Set up a concentrate tank and metering with fully dissolved, particle-free solution.[14]
  5. Monitor sulfide, sulfate, nitrate, nitrite and ammonium at producers and topsides; watch for nitrite or nitrate breakthrough.[2][6][8]
  6. Adjust. Raise dose if sulfide rebounds; cut or add inhibitors if nitrite breakthrough and pitting rise.[8]

What should be monitored?

The cited studies track sulfide and sulfate, nitrate and nitrite (by ion chromatography),[9] ammonium to see the nitrate pathway,[6] microbial community by DNA sequencing,[9] and corrosion by general rate and pitting density rather than rate alone.[8] Nitrite appearing in produced fluids is a signal to check both souring control (good) and pitting risk (bad).

When calcium nitrate is NOT the right choice

  • Systems with high toluene or other nitrate-consuming organics, or nitrogen-limited and electron-donor-rich waters, where nitrate can be consumed before it suppresses SRB or can even stimulate them.[3][7]
  • Topsides and produced-water systems with corrosion sensitivity unless pitting is monitored; nitrite breakthrough raised pitting density.[8]
  • Anyone expecting a universal dose. Reported nitrate figures span more than two orders of magnitude between systems; test your own water and oil.[1][4][13]
  • Reservoir injection with an off-the-shelf fertilizer product. Injection water needs particle-free, specified solution; confirm injectivity, chloride, metals and scaling compatibility first.[14]

Frequently asked questions

How does nitrate injection control H2S in oil reservoirs?

It stimulates nitrate-reducing bacteria that out-compete sulfate-reducing bacteria for organics and oxidise sulfide, and the nitrite they make inhibits SRB. Modelling work ranks nitrite inhibition as the major mechanism.[1][4][10]

What nitrate dose is used for reservoir souring control?

There is no single number. A patent background cites roughly 25–500 ppm nitrate plus nitrite in injected water,[13] bioreactors needed 4–10 mM (about 250–620 mg/L),[1][4] and 25 mg/L failed in one Brazilian column study.[7] Dose is set by trial on the actual water and oil.

Has calcium nitrate been used in a real oil field?

Yes. Statoil used calcium nitrate on Gullfaks, where it replaced 240,000 litres per year of biocide and had run for more than three years by December 2005; Veslefrikk and Statfjord also switched.[21]

Why does nitrate sometimes fail to stop souring?

Nitrate can be consumed by oil components such as toluene,[3] and where organics exceed nitrate demand and nitrogen is limiting it may not inhibit SRB.[7] Low-salinity conditions also did not accumulate nitrite in Bakken tests.[2]

Does nitrate treatment cause corrosion?

Not always, but there is risk. In FPSO topside microcosms nitrate and nitrite treatment lowered general corrosion rates, but nitrite breakthrough gave the highest pitting density.[8]

Can calcium nitrate be dosed into biogas digesters to cut H2S?

Proceedings report higher methane yield and lower H2S with calcium nitrate dosing,[19] and a separate WEF nitrate-recycle sludge process reports at least 90% H2S reduction (not calcium nitrate dosing).[20] Evidence is limited and partly vendor-linked; iron salts and microaeration are the better-documented options.[15]

What are the alternatives to nitrate for sulfide control?

Biocides such as glutaraldehyde or THPS, perchlorate, H2S scavengers, iron salts, microaeration and biogas scrubbing. Each has limits; perchlorate did not work in one heavy-oil test.[4][15][22]
