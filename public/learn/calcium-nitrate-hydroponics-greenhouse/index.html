Calcium nitrate in hydroponics and greenhouse nutrition: targets, stock tanks and how much to dose

Last updated: 7 October 2026  ·  Reviewed by Sujay Shrivastava - Digital Transformation Consultant  ·  21 cited sources (16 high-authority)
In summary

Calcium nitrate (15.5-0-0 plus about 19% calcium) is the standard calcium source in hydroponics because it is highly soluble and supplies calcium and nitrate nitrogen together; its companion ion is nitrate, a nutrient, rather than sulphate or chloride.[1][19] University recipes put the finished nutrient solution at roughly 90 mg/L Ca and 150 mg/L N for lettuce,[8] 144–200 mg/L Ca and 90–190 mg/L nitrate-N for tomato,[2] and about 52 mg/L Ca and 77 mg/L N for strawberry.[12] Calcium nitrate must be kept in a separate concentrate tank from sulphates and phosphates, otherwise calcium sulphate and calcium phosphate precipitate.[4][7][11] As a worked example, 4 mmol/L Ca (160 mg/L) needs about 84–87 g of calcium nitrate per 100 L of final solution, assuming 18.5–19% Ca.

Why is calcium nitrate the standard calcium source in hydroponics?

A hydroponic crop needs calcium at a high concentration, about 90–220 mg/L in the recipes below (roughly 4.5–11 meq/L, our conversion),[2][3][7][8][17] and it needs nitrogen mostly as nitrate. Calcium nitrate supplies both from one salt. Its calcium is released as free Ca2+ without a companion anion the plant does not need: the nitrate is itself a nutrient, unlike the sulphate in gypsum or the chloride in calcium chloride.[3][18] It is also very soluble (about 1,290 g/L for the tetrahydrate at 20 °C), so concentrated stock tanks are practical.[19] For these reasons Wikipedia describes it as popular in the greenhouse and hydroponics trades,[19] and university recipes from Florida, Cornell, Ohio State and Arizona all rely on it as the calcium source.[2][3][4][7][11]

What is in fertilizer-grade calcium nitrate?

The fertilizer-grade product is not pure Ca(NO3)2. It is a double salt with ammonium nitrate and water, 5Ca(NO3)2·NH4NO3·10H2O, graded 15.5-0-0 with 19% calcium.[19] Working the stoichiometry from that formula (molar mass about 1,080.6 g/mol) gives 18.5% Ca and 15.55% N, of which about 14.3 points are nitrate-N and about 1.3 points ammonium-N. This is our own calculation, and it explains why suppliers quote 18.5–19% Ca. Liquid calcium nitrate (7-0-0-11) also exists and is sometimes preferred because it is already clear.[3][4]

Recipes are often written for a different form of the salt, so check which one before you weigh anything:

To convert a recipe, multiply the weight it lists by (Ca% of the listed form ÷ Ca% of your product). A recipe written for the tetrahydrate needs about 8–10% less of an 18.5–19% double salt for the same calcium.

What EC and pH should a calcium nitrate nutrient solution have?

  • Tomato: EC about 2.0 dS/m in the first stage, rising to about 2.4 dS/m in stage 3; pH 5.5–6.5.[2] The Arizona CEAC course notes give a typical EC of about 2.0–3.5 mS/cm (range 1.2–4) for fruiting crops and a pH of about 6.[11]
  • Lettuce: EC about 1.5 and pH 5.5–6.0 (Cornell leafy-greens recipe); the Cornell lettuce handbook calls pH 5.8 optimum with 5.6–6.0 acceptable.[8][9] UF/IFAS cites 1.2 dS/m for germination and 1.5–1.8 dS/m in production for NFT lettuce.[5]
  • Strawberry: EC 0.75–1.25 dS/m, pH 5.5–6.5.[12]

EC is only a measure of total salts. Florida extension warns that holding EC constant while switching to a different fertilizer can hide a deficiency, and that raising EC raises every nutrient at once when only one may be short.[4] Subtract your source water’s own EC when you read the meter.[9]

Why must calcium nitrate go in a separate tank (Tank A/B)?

Calcium reacts with sulphate to form calcium sulphate and with phosphate to form calcium phosphates. At the strength of a stock tank this produces precipitate that clogs emitters and removes nutrients from the crop.[1][4] The Arizona CEAC puts the threshold at concentrates stronger than about 50×.[11] In the dilute final solution, the ions stay in solution, so the two stocks can safely meet only after injection.[10]

Typical split, from the published recipes:

  • Tank A (calcium side): calcium nitrate, often with potassium nitrate and iron chelate.[4][7][11]
  • Tank B (everything else): the phosphate source, magnesium sulphate and micronutrients, sometimes with potassium chloride or part of the potassium nitrate.[4][7]
  • Acid: if source water needs acidifying, Cornell advises adding acid to the tank without calcium nitrate, or using a separate acid tank.[7][11]

Calcium can also be lost in the root zone when pH drifts too high: Sambo et al. note that phosphate availability falls above about pH 7 through precipitation with Ca (minerals such as hydroxylapatite), that Ca-sulphate minerals can limit sulphur, and that Ca and Mg can precipitate as carbonates at high pH and high dissolved CO2.[14]

How do you make a calcium nitrate stock solution?

The maths is a straight ratio. UF/IFAS shows it for 150 mg/L Ca: 150 mg Ca ÷ 0.19 = 790 mg calcium nitrate per litre of final solution; × 100 for a 100× stock = 79 g per litre of stock, or 16.5 lb in a 25-gallon tank.[4] The Arizona CEAC does the same sum for 141 mg/L Ca (170 target minus 29 from source water) at 19% Ca.[11] The University of Hawaii Kratky recipe is a simpler small-scale case: 1 lb/gal calcium nitrate as Stock B, 25 lb in 25 gallons, added in equal volume to Stock A to reach EC 1.5 mS.[10]

How much calcium nitrate per 100 litres? A worked example

Assumption: HRSU grade, 18.5–19% Ca and 15.5% min N. Target: 4.0 mmol/L Ca, soft or RO water with no Ca of its own, 100 L of final solution.

  1. Calcium in mg/L: 4.0 mmol/L × 40.08 mg/mmol = 160.3 mg/L.
  2. Calcium needed for 100 L: 160.3 mg/L × 100 L = 16.03 g Ca.
  3. Product at 19% Ca: 16.03 g ÷ 0.19 = 84.4 g. At 18.5% Ca: 16.03 ÷ 0.185 = 86.7 g.
  4. Nitrogen delivered at 15.5% N: about 131–134 mg/L total N, of which roughly 92% (about 120–124 mg/L) is nitrate-N.
  5. For a 1:100 stock, dissolve the same weight per 1 litre of stock: about 84–87 g/L, well inside the salt’s solubility.[19]

If your water already contains calcium, subtract it first. With 40 mg/L Ca in the source water, the 160 mg/L target needs only 120 mg/L from the fertilizer, or about 63–65 g per 100 L. Florida reports well-water Ca of 40–60 ppm, though not all of it may be available to the crop.[3] This method matches the UF/IFAS worked example (150 mg/L Ca at 19% = 790 mg/L of product).[4] Ratio N:Ca of about 0.8 from this salt alone is why a separate nitrate source such as potassium nitrate is often added to reach 150–190 mg/L N.[4][7]

How does calcium actually reach the plant?

Calcium moves mostly with water in the transpiration stream, up the xylem, and does not move back out of leaves to feed new growth.[6][7] At night, when transpiration is low, root pressure can push calcium into fruits.[6] Pepper fruits transpire less than leaves because of their waxy skin, so leaves compete with fruit for calcium.[6] Two practical results follow:

  • Humidity and airflow matter as much as the recipe. UF/IFAS notes that periods of high humidity can cause lettuce tipburn because leaves do not transpire fast enough to move calcium to the expanding leaf edges.[4] Cornell uses downward paddle fans to raise transpiration and prevent tipburn at high light; without airflow their lettuce had to be grown at a lower light integral.[9]
  • Antagonism from potassium. High K can interfere with Ca uptake and cause blossom-end rot, especially early in the season and when well-water Ca is under about 50 ppm.[3]

Foliar calcium chloride is a rescue tool, not a substitute for correct root-zone calcium: a HortScience trial on NFT lettuce (Ohio State University with USDA-ARS) tested foliar CaCl2 at 0, 200, 400 and 800 mg/L Ca. Twice-weekly sprays at 400 or 800 mg/L gave good tipburn control with minor yield impact, whereas lowering solution EC to 1.4 mS/cm also reduced tipburn in ‘Green Butter’ but cut its yield by 75 g per head compared with EC 1.8.[13]

How does water quality change the recipe?

  • Test first. Cornell says to begin with a lab analysis for alkalinity, EC and specific elements.[7] Florida recommends testing pH, carbonates, S, Mg, Ca and Fe.[3]
  • Alkalinity. Reported as ppm CaCO3, it can range from near 0 (RO water) to over 300 ppm; the higher it is, the more the solution pH rises.[7] UF/IFAS flags bicarbonate above about 30–60 ppm and recommends acidifying to pH 5.6–6.0 with nitric, phosphoric or sulphuric acid.[4]
  • Existing calcium and magnesium. Count them. Arizona subtracts source-water Ca before dosing,[11] and Cornell advises supplying Ca, Mg and S only if the water does not already cover the recipe.[7]
  • Closed systems need clean water. Cornell suggests source EC below 0.25 mS/cm and sodium and chloride ideally below 50 and 70 ppm, which often means reverse osmosis.[7]
  • Carbonate precipitation. At high pH and high dissolved CO2, Ca and Mg can precipitate as carbonates.[14]

What are the most common mistakes?

  1. Mixing the concentrates. Putting calcium nitrate and magnesium sulphate or phosphate in one concentrate tank.[4][11]
  2. Ignoring water calcium and alkalinity, which leads to over- or under-dosing and pH drift.[4][11]
  3. Too much nitrogen early. Keeping tomato N near 60–70 ppm early prevents bullish growth, which can bring blossom-end rot.[3]
  4. Too much potassium relative to calcium, particularly with pre-mixes.[3]
  5. Managing by EC alone, instead of testing individual nutrients; Florida suggests flushing sump tanks weekly and rebuilding the solution.[4]
  6. Letting sludge build up in the calcium tank; anti-caking additives in some dry fertilizers are insoluble and settle out, so rinse tanks periodically.[4]
  7. Expecting nutrient fixes alone to cure tipburn or BER. Humidity, airflow and irrigation consistency matter.[4][6][9]

When calcium nitrate is NOT the right choice

  • Do not mix calcium nitrate with sulphate or phosphate fertilizers in a concentrate. They form calcium sulphate and calcium phosphate precipitate that blocks emitters.[4][11]
  • It is not a complete fertilizer. It gives Ca and N only, with no P, K, Mg, S or micronutrients; the Ca:N ratio of the salt also cannot be changed, so a second N source is usually needed.[4][7]
  • Do not rely on it for tipburn or blossom-end rot alone. Calcium reaches growing tissue by transpiration, so humidity, airflow, root health and EC strategy all affect it.[4][6][9]
  • The recipe numbers are starting points, not requirements. UF/IFAS notes that no single formulation suits every system and climate, and that nutrient concentrations must rise when irrigation is less frequent.[3]
  • Handle it as an oxidiser and store it dry. It is hygroscopic and absorbs moisture from air.[19] See the safety, handling and storage guide.

Frequently asked questions

What is calcium nitrate used for in hydroponics?

It is the main source of calcium and a major source of nitrate nitrogen in hydroponic nutrient solutions. Fertilizer grade is 15.5-0-0 with about 19% Ca, and it is kept in a separate stock tank from sulphates and phosphates.[1][4][19]

How much calcium nitrate per litre of hydroponic solution?

For 150 mg/L Ca, a 19% Ca product needs about 0.79 g per litre (79 g per 100 L); for 160 mg/L Ca it is about 0.84–0.87 g/L. Subtract any calcium already in your water. The calculation is calcium target ÷ Ca fraction of the product.[4][11]

What is the calcium ppm for hydroponic tomatoes and lettuce?

Ohio State lists 144, 160 and 200 mg/L Ca for three tomato stages, and Florida uses 150 mg/L throughout. Cornell’s lettuce recipe uses 90 mg/L Ca with 150 mg/L N.[2][3][8]

Why can’t I mix calcium nitrate with Tank B fertilizers?

Calcium combines with phosphate and sulphate to form insoluble calcium phosphate and calcium sulphate. In stock solutions stronger than about 50 times, this precipitate clogs equipment and strips nutrients, so the two are only combined after dilution.[4][11]

What EC and pH should I run with calcium nitrate?

Tomato: EC about 2.0–2.4 dS/m and pH 5.5–6.5 (OSU). Lettuce: EC about 1.5 and pH 5.5–6.0 (Cornell). Strawberry: EC 0.75–1.25 dS/m.[2][8][12]

Can I use calcium chloride or gypsum instead of calcium nitrate?

Calcium chloride (about 36% Ca) can top up calcium, but supplies no nitrate and adds chloride.[3][7] Gypsum is only sparingly soluble (about 2.4 g/L), which rules out concentrated stocks.[18] Neither supplies nitrogen.

Why do my plants show blossom-end rot or tipburn when the solution has enough calcium?

Calcium travels with transpiration water, so high humidity, low airflow, excess potassium or erratic watering can cause deficiency symptoms even when the root-zone supply is adequate.[3][4][6][9]

Does water alkalinity affect calcium nitrate dosing?

Yes. Count the Ca already in your water, and manage alkalinity with acid. UF/IFAS flags bicarbonate above about 30–60 ppm, and Cornell advises adding acid to the tank that does not contain calcium nitrate.[4][7]
