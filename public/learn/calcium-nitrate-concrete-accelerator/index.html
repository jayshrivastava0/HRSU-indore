Can calcium nitrate be used as a concrete accelerator or antifreeze admixture?

Last updated: 7 October 2026  ·  Reviewed by Sujay Shrivastava - Digital Transformation Consultant  ·  22 cited sources (18 high-authority)
In summary

Yes. Calcium nitrate is a chloride-free set accelerator for Portland cement and is used in cold-weather concreting.[1][4][15] In a Lithuanian study on CEM I 42.5 R concrete, doses of 0.5–3% by cement mass shortened initial setting time at +20 °C from 149 minutes to 120 minutes (1%), 105 minutes (2%) and 45 minutes (3%).[1] The same study found the best 2-day strength gains at 1% (+34% at +5 °C) and, at −5 °C, 3% gave about 2.5 times the 28-day strength of untreated concrete cured the same way.[1] It is mainly a set accelerator, not a strong hardening accelerator at 1 day and 20 °C.[4] It does not prevent freezing and the optimum dose must be found by trial batches.[1][19]

How does calcium nitrate accelerate cement hydration?

Cement set is controlled mainly by two clinker phases. Alite (C3S) dissolves and releases Ca2+ and OH−; when the pore solution becomes supersaturated in calcium hydroxide (portlandite) it crystallises and triggers a burst of renewed alite hydration. The aluminate phase (C3A) reacts with gypsum to form ettringite and later converts to monosulfate (AFm).[4]

  • C3S side. Calcium nitrate dissolves quickly and adds Ca2+ to the mixing water, so less alite has to dissolve before calcium hydroxide supersaturation and crystallisation begin. This shortens the induction period and the setting time.[4] A calorimetry study measured a control paste induction period of about 3 hours, falling to about 2 hours (1%), 1 hour (2%) and almost none (3%).[1]
  • C3A side. Nitrate ions react with aluminate to form nitrate-AFm in addition to ettringite at early age, and the extra hydrates fill pores.[3] A cement containing C3A is significantly affected by calcium nitrate.[1]
  • Side effect on heat. In an insulated paste test the peak hydration temperature fell from 91.9 °C (control) to 67.9 °C at 3% dose, so reaction starts earlier but peaks lower.[1]

What dosage of calcium nitrate is used in concrete (% by cement mass)?

Published laboratory work spans 0.5–3% by cement mass for set acceleration.[1][2] There is no single “standard” dose: the best value depends on cement, temperature and the target. In the Lithuanian concrete series (310 kg/m3 CEM I 42.5 R, w/c 0.55, polycarboxylate superplasticizer) the strength-optimal dose fell as temperature rose.[1]

A separate white Portland cement study (0, 1, 2, 3% dose) found the 1% dose cut initial setting time to 76% of the 275-minute control and raised 28-day strength from 35 to 44 MPa, while higher doses lowered 28-day strength.[2] Two practical points follow: more is not always better, and the 1-day and 28-day optima differ. Regulatory-style performance limits, not a dose, define a commercial accelerator: EN 934-2 requires at least 120% of reference strength at 1 day (20 °C) and 130% at 2 days (5 °C), with initial set at least 30 minutes at 20 °C and at most 60% of the reference at 5 °C.[1][18]

How much does it change set time and strength at 1, 3, 7 and 28 days?

  • 1 day. At 20 °C calcium nitrate is not a hardening accelerator. In slag-blended mortar it gave lower 1-day strength at 20 °C, though concrete cured at +5 °C had higher 1-day strength.[4]
  • 2 days. Gains are largest here and largest at cool temperatures (+34% at +5 °C, +46% at 0 °C in the concrete series).[1]
  • 3 days. I found no sourced Portland-cement concrete data for exactly 3 days; slag-blended mortar showed higher strength from day 3 onwards.[4]
  • 7 days. +12.9% at 1% and 20 °C;[1] white-cement specimens rose from 29 MPa to about 37–38 MPa at 1–2%.[2]
  • 28 days. Gains shrink (+6% to +11% in the concrete series) because calcium nitrate alone has little effect on long-term strength.[1] Some work reports a longer-term benefit for CEM I.[1][4]

Does calcium nitrate protect concrete from freezing?

Only partly. Fresh concrete freezes at about −4 °C and frozen early-age concrete can lose more than 50% of potential strength; it should be protected until it reaches about 3.5 MPa.[19] Calcium nitrate speeds the strength gain needed to reach that point and depresses the freezing point of mix water, but the effect has a limit: in the Lithuanian tests −10 °C behaved as a eutectic limit and no dose (0.5–3%) reached 3.5 MPa in 2 days without extra measures, while at −5 °C 0.5% reached 3.7 MPa.[1] NRMCA states plainly that accelerators do not prevent freezing and do not replace protection and curing.[19] Early antifreeze work favoured nitrite-based blends at −5 and −10 °C.[12] Another study notes that larger nitrite/nitrate amounts than usual are used below −10 °C.[3]

What does cold-weather practice look like?

ACI 306R covers cold-weather planning, protection, curing and accelerating or antifreeze admixtures.[11] NRMCA defines cold weather as more than 3 consecutive days with average temperature below 5 °C, recommends placement temperatures of 13 °C (sections under 300 mm), 10 °C (300–900 mm) and 7 °C (900–1800 mm), and notes a 10 °C drop roughly doubles setting time.[19] Saturated concrete that will face freeze-thaw cycles should reach about 24 MPa first.[19] Use air-entrained mixes for freeze-thaw exposure and avoid water curing when freezing is imminent.[19] Calcium nitrate does not change the air content: it stayed 4.6–5.2% at all doses tested, and it slightly raised initial slump.[1]

How do you trial calcium nitrate in a mix?

The steps below reflect the test design of the published studies, not a prescribed dose for your cement.

  1. Pick the cement and record its C3A, since C3A-containing cements are significantly affected.[1]
  2. Prepare Vicat pastes at 0.5%, 1%, 2% and 3% of cement mass at your site temperatures (+20, +5, 0 °C) and read initial and final set.[1]
  3. Cast concrete at those doses with your actual superplasticizer and check slump and air content.[1]
  4. Cure at the expected initial temperature and test strength at 2, 7 and 28 days.[1]
  5. Compare with the EN 934-2 criteria (120% at 1 day/20 °C, 130% at 2 days/5 °C) if you claim an accelerator class.[18]
  6. Continue moist curing after the cold phase, since further water curing was needed for strength gain.[1]

Does calcium nitrate protect rebar or cause alkali-silica problems?

The older HRSU page said nitrate “acts as a corrosion inhibitor”. That needs care. At 3–4% by cement mass, calcium nitrate inhibited chloride-induced rebar corrosion in long-term tests.[5] A 2001 Waterloo thesis, using synthetic pore solution and cement paste, found that none of the tested inhibitors, including commercial calcium nitrate products, delayed the onset of corrosion.[6] Typical accelerator doses are lower than 3–4%, so do not rely on set-acceleration dosing for corrosion protection. See calcium nitrate as a corrosion inhibitor. Separately, laboratory studies show dissolved calcium nitrate reduces alkali-silica reaction expansion by limiting aggregate dissolution; this is research-stage, not a standard ASR specification route.[7][8]

Is calcium nitrate compatible with other admixtures?

Evidence is limited to the mixes tested. The Lithuanian concrete used a polycarboxylate superplasticizer with no air-content penalty,[1] and ACI 212.3R recommends verifying multi-admixture systems by test placement.[10] Calcium nitrate also worked in 50% slag blends, with a 4% dose equalising 7- and 28-day mortar strength between two slags.[4] Always run your own trial with retarders, water reducers and air-entrainers.

When calcium nitrate is NOT the right choice

  • Very low temperatures without protection. At −10 °C no tested dose of 0.5–3% reached the 3.5 MPa frost-safe strength in 2 days.[1]
  • Relying on it for 1-day hardening at 20 °C or for long-term strength. It is chiefly a set accelerator with a small long-term effect.[1][4]
  • Over-dosing. In one study higher doses lowered 28-day strength and flexural strength.[2]
  • Counting on it as a rebar corrosion fix at accelerator doses. The 3–4% inhibitor dose is above normal accelerator use and results are mixed.[5][6]
  • Treating fertilizer-grade powder as a certified admixture. EN 934-2 and ASTM C494 apply to the admixture as tested in concrete, so conformity needs your own testing.[17][18]

Frequently asked questions

Does calcium nitrate accelerate concrete set time?

Yes. At +20 °C a paste with no admixture set initially at 149 minutes versus 120, 105 and 45 minutes with 1%, 2% and 3% calcium nitrate by cement mass.[1]

What dosage of calcium nitrate is used in concrete?

Published lab tests use 0.5–3% by cement mass; the strength-optimal dose was 1% at +20 and +5 °C, 0.5% at 0 °C and 3% at −5 °C. Confirm with trial batches for your cement.[1]

Why use calcium nitrate instead of calcium chloride?

Calcium chloride is the most efficient and cheapest accelerator but chloride causes pitting corrosion of steel; many specifications restrict it.[20][21] Calcium nitrate is chloride-free but less efficient.[15]

Can calcium nitrate be used for cold-weather concreting?

Yes, down to a point. It shortened setting and raised early strength from +5 to −5 °C in tests, but at −10 °C no dose reached the frost-safe strength alone, and it does not replace protection.[1][19]

Is calcium nitrate a corrosion inhibitor in concrete?

At 3–4% by cement mass it inhibited chloride-induced corrosion in long-term tests, but a pore-solution study found no delay in corrosion onset, so evidence is mixed.[5][6]

Does calcium nitrate increase 28-day concrete strength?

Only slightly. In one concrete series the 1% dose gave +6.2% at 28 days at +20 °C, and higher doses lowered 28-day strength in another study.[1][2]

How does calcium nitrate work chemically?

Extra Ca2+ brings forward calcium hydroxide crystallisation and alite hydration, while nitrate reacts with C3A to form AFm and ettringite.[3][4]
