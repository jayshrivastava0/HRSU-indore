Calcium Nitrate Spec Sheet: What to Check Before You Buy

By Sujay Shrivastava, Editorial Lead — HRSU Indore

A calcium nitrate specification decides what you actually pay for: two quotes can differ by 8% on the invoice and by the opposite amount in delivered calcium. The reason is that "calcium nitrate" names at least three different products with different nutrient content, and the price per tonne says nothing about what each tonne delivers. A 15.5-0-0 granular grade carries 19% calcium and 15.5% nitrogen.[1] The pure tetrahydrate carries about 17% calcium and 12% nitrogen, and the anhydrous salt about 24% calcium and 17% nitrogen.[3] This guide shows a procurement manager how to read a specification sheet and certificate of analysis (CoA), convert every quote to cost per kilogram of delivered calcium, and write the six lines into an RFQ that protect a purchase from caking, short-weight nutrient and unusable lots.

This technical guide is specifically tailored for United States industrial operations under EPA and OSHA regulations, addressing the regulatory frameworks, dosage standards, and operational requirements relevant to this market.

Three Products Sold Under One Name

Most RFQ confusion starts with the product definition. Before comparing prices, confirm which chemical form each supplier is quoting.

FormCalcium (Ca)Nitrogen (N)Typical use
Double salt, 15.5-0-0 granular (calcium ammonium nitrate decahydrate)19.0%15.5% (about 14.5% nitrate, 1.0% ammoniacal)[1]Fertigation, fertilizer blending, bulk agriculture
Tetrahydrate, Ca(NO3)2·4H2Oabout 17.0%about 11.9%Industrial processes, ammonium-free applications
Anhydrous, Ca(NO3)2about 24.4%about 17.1%Rare; absorbs moisture from air and converts to the tetrahydrate[3]

The tetrahydrate and anhydrous percentages follow from atomic masses (molecular weights of about 236.15 and 164.09). Water accounts for roughly 30.5% of the tetrahydrate by mass, which is why the same word on a quote can hide a large difference in active content.

Convert Every Quote to Cost per Kilogram of Calcium

A price per tonne compares offers only when the products are identical. The fair comparison for a calcium-driven application is cost per kilogram of calcium delivered:

Cost per kg Ca = price per tonne ÷ (1,000 × Ca fraction)

The worked example below uses illustrative prices to show the arithmetic. Replace them with your own quotes.

OfferPrice per tonneCa contentCost per kg Ca
Offer A$40019.0%$2.11
Offer B$37017.0%$2.18

Offer B is $30 per tonne cheaper and still costs about 3% more per kilogram of calcium. On a 500-tonne annual contract, Offer B looks like a $15,000 saving on the invoice. To deliver the same 95,000 kg of calcium that 500 tonnes of Offer A provides, Offer B needs about 559 tonnes and costs roughly $6,700 more. The same method applies to nitrogen when nitrate nitrogen is the driver.

Six Lines to Check on Every Spec Sheet and CoA

  1. Guaranteed minimum versus typical value. A typical value is an average. A guarantee is a limit you can claim against. Yara's label guarantees 15.5% total nitrogen and 19.0% calcium;[1] Haifa's technical data sheet lists typical values together with minimum and maximum limits for each analyte, for example 15.0 to 15.5% for total nitrogen.[2] Write the guaranteed minimum into the purchase contract, not the typical figure.
  2. Nitrogen split: nitrate versus ammoniacal. The double salt carries about 1% ammoniacal nitrogen alongside roughly 14.5% nitrate nitrogen.[1] Applications that cannot tolerate ammonium, such as certain hydroponic recipes, need that split stated on the CoA.
  3. Moisture and hydration state. Calcium nitrate is hygroscopic. In the tetrahydrate form, published salt data put its deliquescence point at about 50% relative humidity at 25 °C,[3] so a warehouse or port yard above that humidity pulls water from the air. Ask for the moisture limit at dispatch and the packaging specification, because moisture gained in transit becomes caked product.
  4. Water-insoluble matter. For fertigation and liquid blending, insoluble residue clogs filters and drippers. Many datasheets state a solubility claim without a numeric limit, so request a maximum percentage in writing.
  5. Granule size and physical form. Granule size distribution decides whether the product flows through blending equipment and dissolves in a dosing tank. Haifa lists its grade as white granules;[2] ask each supplier for a sieve analysis range.
  6. Lot traceability. The CoA should carry a batch number that matches the number printed on the bag or bulk document, the analysis date and the testing method. A CoA without these is a brochure.

RFQ Language That Protects the Buyer

  • State the exact product form (double salt 15.5-0-0, tetrahydrate or anhydrous) and require the supplier to quote that form only.
  • Require guaranteed minimum Ca and N, plus the nitrate and ammoniacal split.
  • Specify a maximum moisture content at dispatch and the packaging (for example moisture-barrier bags or lined containers).
  • Require a lot-specific CoA with each shipment and a retained sample.
  • Define the remedy: a price adjustment pro rata for nutrient shortfall, and the right to reject lots outside the guaranteed range.

What This Means for the Decision-Maker

For executives approving a sourcing contract, the specification discipline above is a cost-control measure. A contract written on a delivered-nutrient basis removes the supplier's ability to win a tender on a lower-content product, and it gives the company a documented basis for claims when a lot arrives off-spec. Procurement teams that standardise this RFQ template across sites also shorten supplier qualification, because every bidder answers the same six questions.
