Indexing a Calcium Nitrate Contract to Nitric Acid: A Buyer's Guide to Price Spikes

By Sujay Shrivastava, Editorial Lead — HRSU Indore

In October 2026 HRSU's calcium nitrate price index rose 18.5% in one month after two Indian nitric acid producers revised prices. In the six months before that, the same index fell from 150.0 in April to 98.3 in July, a drop of 34%. A buyer on a fixed-price contract signed at the April level paid the highest price of the year for three months. A buyer on a contract that tracked nitric acid paid less every month of that fall and absorbed the October rise in the same proportion it had been saving. This guide explains how an acid-indexed calcium nitrate contract works, what the formula looks like, which clauses protect both parties, and when a fixed price is still the right choice.

This technical guide is specifically tailored for United States industrial operations under EPA and OSHA regulations, addressing the regulatory frameworks, dosage standards, and operational requirements relevant to this market.

Why Calcium Nitrate Prices Move With Nitric Acid

Calcium nitrate is made by reacting limestone with nitric acid: CaCO3 + 2 HNO3 → Ca(NO3)2 + CO2 + H2O. Nitric acid is the largest material input. The stoichiometry fixes how much of it each kilogram of product consumes:

  • Tetrahydrate, Ca(NO3)2·4H2O (236.15 g/mol): 2 × 63.01 ÷ 236.15 = 0.534 kg of 100% HNO3 per kg of product.
  • Anhydrous, Ca(NO3)2 (164.09 g/mol): 2 × 63.01 ÷ 164.09 = 0.768 kg of 100% HNO3 per kg of product.

These are stoichiometric minimums. Real consumption runs slightly higher because no plant converts at 100% yield, and nitric acid is bought as a 55–68% solution, not as 100% acid. The ratio still tells you the sensitivity: every unit the acid price moves shifts the cost of a kilogram of tetrahydrate by roughly half a unit. Neither buyer nor supplier gains from a contract that guesses the direction of a volatile input.

How HRSU Passes Through Acid Changes

HRSU reflects spot changes in nitric acid directly in the price of calcium nitrate, in both directions. The price does not wait for older acid stock to work through the plant. If the acid price falls, the product price falls by the corresponding amount, and if it rises, the product price rises by the same logic. This applies to established accounts that have an ordering relationship with HRSU. A new account starts on the price quoted at onboarding and moves onto pass-through as the working relationship develops.

The effect is a price that follows the input rather than a supplier's margin forecast. A buyer sees the cost of the acid move through to the invoice, and sees the same mechanism work in the buyer's favour when the acid falls.

The Formula: How to Write an Acid-Indexed Price

An indexed price has three parts: a base price, a reference for the input, and a conversion factor.

P(t) = P(0) + k x [ A(t) - A(0) ]

P(0)  base price per kg at signing
A(0)  reference nitric acid price per kg at signing
A(t)  reference nitric acid price per kg at the review date
k     kg of acid consumed per kg of product
      tetrahydrate, 100% HNO3 basis: 0.534
      (divide by the acid strength if A is quoted for 60% acid: 0.534 / 0.60 = 0.89)

Worked example with illustrative figures. Take a nitric acid benchmark of USD 0.36/kg (the global figure cited in the October Market Pulse) and tetrahydrate at k = 0.534. The acid cost carried inside each kilogram of product is 0.534 × 0.36 = USD 0.192. A 25% rise in acid adds USD 0.048 per kg, which is USD 48,000 on a 1,000 MT annual volume. A 25% fall removes the same amount. A fixed-price buyer carries that swing as an unpriced risk, and the supplier prices a premium into the fixed quote to cover it.

Six Clauses That Make an Indexed Contract Work

  1. A named reference. Name the acid price series or the supplier's published index that A(t) comes from, and the date convention. A formula that references nothing public becomes a dispute.
  2. Both directions. The price must fall when the input falls. Write it in. A one-way clause is a price increase with extra steps.
  3. A review band. Reprice only when the input moves more than a stated percentage from the last reference, for example 3%. This removes noise and keeps invoices predictable.
  4. A review frequency. Monthly review fits a volatile year. Quarterly suits a stable one.
  5. A cap and a floor (optional). A collar gives the finance team a budget range. It costs some of the downside benefit, and it is the standard tool when a board needs a number.
  6. Evidence on request. The supplier shows the input movement that triggered a change. Transparency is what makes the formula credible, and it protects the supplier from accusations of opportunism.

When a Fixed Price Is Still the Right Choice

An indexed contract trades budget certainty for fair pricing. A fixed price remains correct for a single spot shipment, for short horizons of one or two months, and for buyers whose own customers contract on fixed prices and who cannot pass changes through. For annual volume, the choice is between a supplier-priced risk premium inside a fixed quote and a visible formula.

What This Means for Executives

A fixed-price contract on a volatile input hands the supplier an option for free: if the input rises, the supplier is squeezed; if it falls, the supplier keeps the difference. A squeezed supplier cuts service first, through delayed loadings, tighter quantities or a quiet request to reopen terms. A formula removes that pressure and turns a supply risk into a budget line you can forecast. Finance teams budget the base price, add a sensitivity range using the conversion factor above, and review monthly against the published index.

How Does an Indexed Calcium Nitrate Contract Protect Buyers When Prices Fall?

An indexed contract reprices downward whenever the reference input falls beyond the review band. In 2026 HRSU's price index fell 34% from April to July. A buyer on an indexed contract captured that fall as it happened, while a buyer on a fixed price set in April paid the April level through the whole decline.

How Much Nitric Acid Does One Kilogram of Calcium Nitrate Consume?

One kilogram of calcium nitrate tetrahydrate consumes a stoichiometric minimum of 0.534 kg of 100% nitric acid. One kilogram of anhydrous calcium nitrate consumes 0.768 kg. Real consumption runs slightly higher because of process yield, and acid is bought as a 55–68% solution, so the factor for a quoted acid strength is the 100% figure divided by that strength.
